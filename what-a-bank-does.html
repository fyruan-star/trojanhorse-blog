WhatA Bank Does

ON ADVICE, MONEY, AND THE DIFFERENCE
BETWEEN THE TWO CHAIRS AT THE TABLE

Francis Ruan

An investment bank does two things that look like one thing. It gives advice, and it arranges money. Almost everything written about the industry, including the funny parts, is about the hours rather than the work, so here is the work.

Advice, which means running the process

When a company is sold, somebody has to run the sale. Decide who to approach, in what order, with what information, on what timetable. Build the model that says what the thing is worth. Manage a room of people who each want a different outcome, including the founder, who is about to make a decision they will think about for the rest of their life.

That is sell-side. The bank is paid a percentage when it closes, and close to nothing if it does not, which tells you most of what you need to know about the incentives in the room.

Buy-side is the other chair. You represent the acquirer: what to pay, how to structure it, how to not overpay in an auction designed to make you overpay.

Money, which is the half nobody writes about

The other business is arranging the money, and in a decade like this one it is arguably the more interesting half.

A company that wants to grow can sell shares, which means giving away a slice of itself forever, or borrow, which means promising to pay it back. Banks arrange both, and the choice between them is one of the most revealing things a company ever does.

Equity is ownership. An IPO is the first public sale of it. Debt is a loan. A convertible note is a loan allowed to change its mind and become shares, which is why companies like it, the interest is cheap, and why lenders like it, because if the stock runs they stop being a lender and start being an owner. A term loan B is a large floating-rate loan chopped up and sold to funds rather than held by one bank. The B is where it sits in the queue if things go wrong. There is an A. Nobody talks about the A.

Coverage, and why the word exists

Banks organise themselves by industry, and a coverage group is the team that owns the relationship with companies in one of them. Technology coverage knows the semiconductor chief executives, has read every filing, and is in the room before there is a transaction to be in the room for.

The job is substantially about being known. You cannot pitch your way into a founder’s decision in the week they make it. You are either already the person they call or you are not, and that relationship took eleven years to build.

Which is why banks hire bankers rather than train them, and why a senior move between firms is reported like a transfer window. They are not buying the person’s process. They are buying the phone.

What the money is for

The fee is a percentage and the percentage falls as the deal gets larger. On something enormous it can be a fraction of a percent, which still runs to tens of millions, which is the part everyone finds outrageous and is also the part that is genuinely contested on every deal.

Whether it is worth it is a real question and I am not going to pretend it is settled. What I will say is that the mistakes are expensive in a way that is hard to see from outside. A company sold at the wrong moment, or to the wrong buyer, or on terms that unravel in the regulatory review, costs its owners vastly more than the fee. Several of the toppled columns on the timeline are that mistake, in public, at scale.

Strip the hours and the shirts away and the job is this: somebody has to decide what a thing is worth, and somebody has to find the money. Everything else is the apparatus around those two sentences.
