The first horse: private credit partnerships

Cuckoos Grow Up In Nests They Did Not Build

Private credit could help competing institutions build the relationships that lead to advisory mandates.

In September 2024, Citi and Apollo announced a direct lending program expected to finance approximately $25 billion of debt opportunities. Citi’s banking head, Viswas Raghavan, said the partnership would give clients “a range of options to meet their evolving financing needs.” Apollo’s co-president, Jim Zelter, described the opportunity to “tap into Citi’s extensive client relationships.” The announcement called it a “win-win arrangement.”

Those statements explain the immediate commercial logic. Citi contributes client access and banking expertise. Apollo contributes capital. Together, they can offer financing that either might find harder to provide alone.

They also identify what could make the arrangement consequential for advisory work. Apollo gains access to relationships that Citi has spent years developing.

That matters because financing can influence the choice of an adviser. A 2002 Federal Reserve Bank of New York paper found that “acquirers tend to choose their own banks” as merger advisers. The researchers connected that choice to access to loans that could finance the acquisition. The study concerns a historical sample, but it provides evidence that lending and advisory relationships can reinforce one another.

Consider a software company borrowing to fund acquisitions. Its lender reviews financial performance, discusses borrowing capacity, and evaluates proposed purchases. Over time, those interactions can give the lender knowledge of the business and access to its decision-makers.

If that institution develops additional capabilities, it can compete for more assignments. KKR’s capital markets business already provides financing services to “both our portfolio companies and independent clients.” Capital markets execution differs from M&A advisory, but it demonstrates that an alternative asset manager can build a financial services business extending beyond its own investments.

My argument is that banks should treat these partnerships as decisions about future competition as well as current financing capacity. A partner supplying capital today may become qualified to win more of the client’s business tomorrow.

This challenges the assumption that the bank can contribute its relationships while keeping its position as the client’s principal financial adviser. Relationships are strengthened through repeated useful interactions. A financing partner participating in those interactions has an opportunity to establish its own standing with management.

That is how private credit could function as a Trojan Horse. The bank welcomes an outside institution because its capital improves the bank’s offering. The same arrangement gives that institution access to clients it might otherwise struggle to reach. The potential threat enters through a service the bank has chosen to provide.

There is a strong counterargument. Partnerships can help banks retain clients they might otherwise lose, and the bank may remain responsible for the relationship. A borrower can also choose separate lenders and advisers, particularly when it wants independent advice. Access alone does not guarantee that a private credit manager will win an advisory mandate.

The outcome depends on who becomes more valuable to the client over time. I would therefore watch whether these partnerships produce repeat business for the bank, the capital provider, or both, and whether the capital provider begins winning assignments independently.
