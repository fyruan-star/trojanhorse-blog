TheMargin Gap

FIFTY NINE IS THE NUMBER IN
THE DECK, EIGHT IS THE BUSINESS

Francis Ruan

Direct to consumer brands love quoting gross margin, and I have started treating that as a small act of misdirection. Huel's is fifty nine percent, which sounds like a software company wearing an apron. Its adjusted EBITDA margin in the same year was about eight. Both numbers are true, only one gets said out loud, and the gap between them is the entire reason Danone bought the company.

Run the two lines yourself

I train for a half Ironman and I race HYROX, so complete nutrition is a category I watch as a person and not only as somebody reading filings. Danone announced the acquisition in March 2026 at around eight hundred and sixty million pounds and the CMA cleared it that August.

In the financial year to 2024 Huel recorded two hundred and fourteen million pounds of revenue at a fifty nine percent gross margin, and eighteen point two million pounds of adjusted EBITDA. Revenue reached two hundred and fifty million the following year, up sixteen percent. Fifty nine percent of two hundred and fourteen is about a hundred and twenty six million of gross profit. EBITDA came in at eighteen. So roughly a hundred and eight million pounds, close to half of all revenue, leaves the building somewhere below the gross line.

Which means the category is describing itself wrongly

In a subscription business selling direct, the overwhelming share of that hundred and eight million is the cost of finding the next customer. Huel is not expensive to make. Huel is expensive to sell.

That is why I think the standard framing of this category is off. We talk about DTC brands as though the achievement is the product economics, and the fifty nine percent is genuinely good. But a fifty nine that arrives as an eight is not a product story, it is a customer acquisition story with a product attached, and the whole sector has been quoting the flattering half of that sentence for a decade.

Why I think this synergy is real, which is rare for me

I am ordinarily sceptical of synergy arguments because they are the easiest slide in any deck and the hardest line to find afterwards. This one is different, and the difference is that the mechanism is concrete rather than cultural. Shelf space substitutes directly for paid acquisition. You are not hoping two organisations learn to collaborate. You are swapping a cost line for an asset the buyer already owns.

My read is that Danone did not buy a food company at all. It bought a marketing bill it believes it can stop paying. And that gives a test requiring no model whatsoever: watch whether EBITDA margin climbs from eight toward the gross margin over three years. If it does, the thesis was right. If it has not moved, Danone bought revenue at a food multiple and inherited the advertising budget with it.

My concern

Retail is not free, it is differently expensive, and a brand built on a subscription relationship does not always survive the move to a shelf where it sits beside eleven alternatives. Some of the loyalty in those numbers may be a function of the format rather than the formula.

That is a hypothesis, not a finding. It is also why I would watch subscriber retention as closely as I watch the margin, because those two lines moving in opposite directions would tell you the trade went through and the asset did not.

My call

The best articulated thesis on my list and the easiest to grade, which are related virtues. Fifty nine into eight is the whole deal. Either Danone closes that gap, or it did not need to own the company to find out.

Fifty nine percent arriving as eight is not a food business. It is customer acquisition with a product attached.
