Trojan Horse of the WeekWeek of 26 October 2026

Can P&G Keep Thorne’s Customers Loyal?

P&G is paying $3.8 billion for a brand whose value is that people trust it. That trust was not part of the negotiation, the people who hold it mostly do not follow mergers and acquisitions, and the record supports both outcomes.

The asset that does not transfer

The price in this deal assumes that Thorne’s customers keep buying after the ownership changes. P&G agreed on 4 August 2026 to pay $3.8 billion, about five and a half times the roughly $680 million L Catterton paid in 2023, for a company that Marketing Dive reports passed $500 million in annual revenue. The prices and dates are set out in the earlier piece on this deal.

A factory, a patent or a lease changes hands when the contract is signed. Customer loyalty does not. It belongs to the customers, none of whom signed anything, and it can leave without an announcement. That is the Trojan horse in this transaction. Loyalty is the reason the price is what it is, so it is the part of the purchase most welcomed, and it is also the part the buyer cannot take delivery of.

Who will decide

Almost nobody who decides this follows mergers and acquisitions. The deal is discussed among bankers, investors and the trade press. A person who buys Thorne magnesium or fish oil learns about a change of ownership, if at all, from the product, so the signals that reach them are the price, the label, how the product works for them and whether the certification printed on the bottle is still there.

Thorne’s own research, as Marketing Dive reports it, suggests that many buyers rely on simple cues. The company found consumers overwhelmed and confused by the market, and found that its messages about scientific rigor mattered less to them than it had assumed. If most customers read the brand through a few visible cues, a change they cannot see, such as a cheaper input, would also pass most of them by. The customers positioned to notice are the minority who can verify, meaning practitioners, athletes and heavy users. A brand with a practitioner heritage relies on that minority for a credibility the wider audience then borrows. That is my reasoning and not a finding, since the sources do not give the split between the two groups.

What people are saying

The worry is specific. A Flow Space roundup of online reaction to the deal reports commenters split. Some hope the deal makes Thorne cheaper and easier to find, and others fear reformulation and lower quality, with some pointing to P&G’s past recalls.

It is the worry that was recorded when P&G bought New Chapter in 2012. About a year afterward Nutritional Outlook quoted John Barrymore, who helped broker that sale, saying consumers worry that an acquirer might change product ingredients to make them cheaper. It is a worry about incentive and not about an announced change, and no change has been announced here. The one statement from Thorne that I could verify is general. Its chief executive, Colin Watts, said the company wants to expand with P&G while keeping to its values and standards (NutraIngredients), and I found no specific commitment on formulation or certification.

What P&G has done before

P&G bought New Chapter in 2012, when its sales were widely reported at about $100 million a year. After a year, Nutritional Outlook reported, P&G had added staff for quality control and innovation, the article noted no change in price, and the brand had not been expanded into other channels despite requests from mass-market retailers. The founder, Paul Schulick, said P&G was keeping its promise to leave the brand standalone. Marc Brush, a consultant and former editor of Nutrition Business Journal, says P&G has done a better job than most buyers of maintaining brand equity in supplement acquisitions.

In 2018 Paul and Barbi Schulick left. They cited “financial pressures to accelerate profits,” and Paul Schulick said that pressure could lead to products losing their distinctiveness (NutraIngredients). That report describes no specific change to New Chapter’s formulation, quality, price or distribution. The feared reformulation therefore does not appear in the record. What appears is the people who gave the brand its credibility leaving six years in, with profit pressure as the stated reason.

The other precedent critics cite is the December 2021 recall of aerosol dry shampoo and dry conditioner sprays sold under six names, among them Pantene, Aussie and Herbal Essences, after benzene was found in some of them. P&G stated that the benzene came from the propellant and not from an ingredient, that the levels detected were not expected to cause adverse health consequences, and that it had received no reports of adverse events (FDA notice). That is a different failure from a cost-driven reformulation. It is a supplied input failing at scale, and it bears on a brand whose reputation rests on testing.

Why it is close to even

The record supports both outcomes. For P&G keeping the customers, it left New Chapter standalone, added quality staff in the first year, and an experienced observer rates it above most buyers. Against, Brush says that at this price Thorne has to keep growing quickly, that most marquee deals decline over time, and that the daily decisions which protect quality and trust are difficult to defend as years pass.

A price that requires fast growth rewards the two things customers fear, which are cutting cost and a faster pace of profit. It also gives P&G a reason to protect the loyalty, since a premium wellness brand does not grow by losing the people who trust it. I cannot tell from the record which incentive will be stronger, which is why I put the outcome close to even.

How a loss would show up

A loss of loyalty would not arrive as a headline. Thorne stopped filing public reports when the 2023 tender closed, and after the sale it will sit inside P&G’s health care business, which P&G reports in aggregate. A fall in repeat purchase would be visible to P&G first, and to outsiders only through third-party sales data or the segment results.

What would settle it

Three things can be checked after the deal closes without access to internal figures. The first is whether Thorne keeps its NSF Certified for Sport certification, which Thorne says covers more than 20 of its products and not its whole range (Thorne). The second is whether prices or pack sizes change on the products existing customers buy most. The third is whether the practitioner relationships that gave the brand its credibility survive, since those customers are the ones able to tell the difference. Jones Day expects the deal to close in the fourth quarter of 2026 (Jones Day).

Prices and dates come from Thorne’s announcement and from the deal record published by P&G’s counsel. The account of consumer reaction rests on a published roundup that I could read only as a search summary, because the original is closed to automated readers, and on trade reporting of the reaction to the earlier New Chapter purchase. I could not read Reddit or other forums directly, so this is not a count of posts. The reasoning about who decides the outcome is mine.
