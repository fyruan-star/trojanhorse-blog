The story of this sector

Start at the buyer's side of the table, because that is where the leverage moved. In October 2025 OpenAI, which has no chip factory, announced two supply deals in a week. AMD agreed to supply 6 gigawatts of GPUs and gave OpenAI a warrant for up to 160 million of its own shares, vesting as the purchases scale (AMD). A week later OpenAI and Broadcom said they would deploy 10 gigawatts of accelerators that OpenAI had designed itself (OpenAI).

Nvidia's own offer had come first, a letter of intent for 10 gigawatts and up to $100 billion of investment. Months later Nvidia's finance chief said “We still haven't completed a definitive agreement,” and Jensen Huang called the pledge “never a commitment” (Fortune, Fortune). Put the three side by side and a buyer's strategy shows. A customer that can threaten to design its own chip gets suppliers to bid with equity. AMD welcomed a very large order and handed over a share of itself to win it, and the risk that came in with the order is a customer that now owns part of its supplier.

Look down the rest of the chart for the bottlenecks. TSMC makes 72.5% of the top ten foundries' revenue. SK hynix holds about half of high-bandwidth memory, though Samsung's share is rising as its next generation ships. ASML is the only company that makes the extreme ultraviolet machines every leading-edge fab needs. Where the chart shows one name at a chokepoint, that company sets terms for everyone above it.

The challengers worth watching change the premise of the row. Cerebras builds one chip from an entire wafer, and went public in May 2026. Substrate is trying to replace the light source in lithography with a particle accelerator that writes with X-rays, and it has money and no revenue. In China, Huawei already ships 28% of AI chips to Nvidia's 34% (IDC, as reported). The orders to watch are the 14A decisions at Intel Foundry in the next two quarters, the first racks of OpenAI's own chips, and the volume ramp of AMD's MI450.

The story of this sector

Every part of this sector is a queue. GE Vernova has gas turbine slots reserved into 2030, with 56 gigawatts of reservations on its books. Dell ended its last fiscal year with a $43 billion backlog of AI servers, and IDC says supply, not demand, is what holds server shipments back. Equinix summed up its quarter with the line that “the vast majority of our largest deals were driven by AI workloads” (Equinix). A queue is a good place to study strategy, because the ones in line are deciding who they are willing to pay to move up.

Money is the clearest example. Meta needed a gigawatt-scale campus in Louisiana and agreed a joint venture in which Blue Owl funds hold 80% and Meta 20%. Meta leases the buildings and provided a guarantee for the first sixteen years (Meta). Meta welcomed that capital because it let the campus be built faster and kept most of the cost off its own balance sheet. The risk that rode in with it is a long guarantee that stays with Meta if demand disappoints. The first horse on the Trojan Horses page describes how private credit enters a bank's relationships in much the same way.

Power gets bought the same way, years ahead. Meta signed three nuclear deals that support up to 6.6 gigawatts and prepaid Oklo, with a first phase targeted for 2030. Amazon's deal with X-energy could reach 5 gigawatts by 2039. A buyer that pays up front to get something built has taken on some of the builder's risk, which is the same arrangement as Renesas's deposit to Wolfspeed (that case).

The networking row holds a quieter shift. Ethernet has moved from under a fifth of AI cluster switch sales to about two-thirds, and Celestica, which builds to the cloud buyers' own designs, led the second quarter ahead of Nvidia. The startup to watch is Ayar Labs, which wants to replace copper links between chips with light. In storage, VAST Data's $30 billion valuation beside DDN's $5 billion shows how far investors will pay for the fastest-growing name in a row with no audited share figure.

The story of this sector

On a September morning Oracle reported that its remaining performance obligations, the contracted revenue it has yet to deliver, had reached $664 billion. In the same release its free cash flow for the quarter was negative $5 billion (Oracle). A company long known for databases has signed the largest AI contracts in the industry and is paying for the buildings before the revenue arrives. Analysts estimate OpenAI is about half of that backlog, so Oracle has made a very large bet on one customer's ability to keep paying.

Google took the opposite route. Its cloud grew 82% in the June quarter and carries a reported $514 billion backlog, and it sells its own chips and its own models alongside the rental. Amazon still leads on share at 28% and grew AWS 37%, its fastest in eighteen quarters. Microsoft's Azure grew 43% and its commercial backlog is reported at $678 billion. The three are growing at the same time, which is unusual, and it tells you the demand behind them is a shortage of capacity and not a contest for customers.

That shortage built a new tier. CoreWeave rents Nvidia systems to the buyers who cannot build quickly enough, and its customers include Meta, OpenAI and Microsoft, which was about 67% of its 2025 revenue. Nebius holds long contracts from Microsoft and Meta. A landlord for the biggest tenants is a strong business while the shortage lasts, and the interest bills show the cost. CoreWeave paid $640 million of net interest in one quarter.

The practice squad is full of companies selling inference, the serving of finished models to developers. Fireworks AI and Together AI both raised at valuations above $8 billion this summer, and Wasabi's no-exit-fee storage shows a different premise, selling the customer's freedom to leave (that case). The test for the whole sector is conversion. A backlog counts promises, and the quarters ahead show how many of them become revenue.

The story of this sector

Visa announced that its payments volume passed $4 trillion in a single quarter for the first time, up 10% at constant dollars, with net revenue of $11.6 billion (Investing.com). Mastercard's gross dollar volume was $2.9 trillion. Both sell the same thing, a fee on each transaction on a network that banks and merchants have no practical way to leave, and both are growing their value-added services faster than their core, 34% and 20%. The cards are the foundation, and the services are where the networks are looking for new revenue.

The pressure on them comes from outside the card. Stablecoin supply is about $316 to $322 billion, with Tether near 58% and Circle's USDC at $73.3 billion at the end of the second quarter (Circle). Circle earns the interest on the reserves behind its coins, and the return rate fell 66 basis points to 3.48% in the quarter, so its revenue grew 7% while average circulation grew 25%. An issuer's income moves with interest rates in a way a card network's does not.

The most dramatic move was a proposal. In mid-July Stripe and Advent were reported to have offered $60.50 a share for PayPal, about $53.4 billion and a 28% premium, financed with $17 billion of equity that includes Block and about $50 billion of bank debt (CNBC). Stripe processed $1.9 trillion in 2025, so the younger company would own the older one jointly with a buyout firm. I found no later reporting on PayPal's response, and the cards say so.

Among the banks, Revolut's reported $115 billion valuation from an employee share sale sits next to public markets valuing listed neobanks at roughly four to five times revenue. Chime's public figures, with revenue of $670 million, up 27%, are the cleanest data point in the row, and it counts active members, which makes it hard to compare with Nubank's registered customers.

Method, and what this does not cover

Every figure on a card carries a period and a link. Sourced means a company filing or release, or a named research firm such as Dell'Oro, IDC, TrendForce, Counterpoint, Synergy or Nielsen. Reported means credible press or an industry tracker without the underlying document in hand. Inferred means the line or the badge is my reading of the structure and no source states it. The lines that say why a company leads and what could change the order are my reading of the evidence on each card.

A starter is a company with a measurable lead in its row. A second-string player has shipping product or named customers. A practice-squad player is private or recently public, with a disclosed funding round or a named transaction. Where sources conflict on a figure, the card shows the range or says that the sources conflict, and a startup whose funding could not be confirmed was left off. Search summaries can paraphrase, so the figures above come from the cited pages and not from memory, and some pages could not be opened directly. Those are marked reported.

Every relationship line was checked against its source on 23 October 2026. Lines whose source did not state the claim were reworded or removed, and relationships that were only my reading are not drawn. Some sources block automated reading, so for those the figures were confirmed through other coverage of the same event.

Not covered. The atlas holds nine sectors, chosen for what a Bay Area technology coverage banker or a tech M&A banker works on. Telecom, robotics, mobility, health technology, defense and space, quantum, energy technology and the materials foundation were researched and then removed. The companies are mostly American and Chinese, and Europe, India, Japan and Korea are thin outside the sectors where they lead.

The full data is in tech-atlas.json, with every card and relationship and its source.
