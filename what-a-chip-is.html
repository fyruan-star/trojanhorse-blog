WhatA Chip Is

THE DIFFERENCE BETWEEN DRAWING ONE
AND BUILDING ONE, AND WHY IT IS A COUNTRY’S PROBLEM

Francis Ruan

A semiconductor is a slab of silicon with a very small city printed on it. Billions of switches, each one either on or off, arranged so that flicking them in the right order does arithmetic. That is the whole idea. Everything after this is logistics.

Three companies, one chip

Here is the thing almost nobody outside the industry knows, and it explains most of the last decade. Designing a chip and making a chip are completely different businesses, run by different companies, in different countries, with different problems.

The architecture is the instruction set. The basic grammar the chip understands: add this, fetch that, compare these two. ARM sells this grammar and does not make anything at all. It licenses the design and takes a royalty on every chip anybody stamps it into, which is roughly thirty billion chips a year.

The design is the actual layout. NVIDIA, Apple and Qualcomm do this. They draw an extraordinarily complicated picture and own no factory whatsoever. The industry calls them fabless, which sounds like an insult and is a business model.

The manufacture is the factory, called a fab, and this is where it gets strange. A leading-edge fab costs upward of twenty billion dollars, takes years to build, and there are effectively three companies on earth that can run one at the smallest sizes. The most advanced of them, TSMC, is in Taiwan.

Why that last sentence keeps governments awake

Read it again. Nearly every advanced chip in the world is made in one place, and that place is the subject of an unresolved territorial dispute.

This is why a committee you have never heard of blocked the largest technology takeover ever attempted, why there are export controls on machines most people could not identify, and why several governments have spent enormous sums trying to build fabs at home. Chips stopped being a component somewhere around 2018 and became a strategic reserve, like oil, except harder to find and considerably harder to make.

The one machine

If you want a single fact that makes the whole field feel absurd, it is this. The machines that print the smallest circuits use extreme ultraviolet light, and exactly one company in the world makes them. ASML, in the Netherlands. Each machine costs a few hundred million dollars and ships in several aeroplanes.

So the supply chain for the most important product of the century runs through one Dutch machine, one Taiwanese factory, and one British instruction set owned by a Japanese holding company. Nobody designed it this way. It simply happened, the way cities happen.

Nobody makes a chip. Three different companies in three different countries each make part of one, and every argument in this section is about which part was worth owning.
