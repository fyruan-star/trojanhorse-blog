The Tech Atlas

17 sectors, 175 sourced relationships and 237 player cards, ranked as a depth chart.

This is a depth chart for the whole technology industry. The idea comes from football, where a coach lists each position and ranks the players who could fill it. Here every row is a position in the stack, such as AI accelerators, foundries or customer service software. Each row has three tiers. The starters are the companies winning today, measured by a number with a date and a source. The second string holds credible challengers, with shipping product or named customers behind them. The practice squad holds startups that have raised real money.

The lines are the part I enjoy most. A solid line joins companies that supply or ally with each other, a dashed line joins rivals, and an arrow runs from an owner or an investor to the company it holds. Click a card to see its lines, including the ones that cross into other sectors, because those usually explain the most. A chip supplier who invests in its own customer is telling you something, and so is a cloud that rents capacity to a rival.

Every number carries its source, and a small tag says how far to trust it. Sourced means a company filing or release, or a named research firm. Reported means credible press coverage without the document in hand. Inferred means the line is my reading of the structure and no source states it. The figures are as of 23 October 2026, and markets in this industry move faster than that date suggests.

The play in this sector

Start at the buyer's side of the table, because that is where the leverage moved. In October 2025 OpenAI, which has no chip factory, announced two supply deals in a week. AMD agreed to supply 6 gigawatts of GPUs and gave OpenAI a warrant for up to 160 million of its own shares, vesting as the purchases scale (AMD). A week later OpenAI and Broadcom said they would deploy 10 gigawatts of accelerators that OpenAI had designed itself (OpenAI).

Nvidia's own offer had come first, a letter of intent for 10 gigawatts and up to $100 billion of investment. Months later Nvidia's finance chief said “We still haven't completed a definitive agreement,” and Jensen Huang called the pledge “never a commitment” (Fortune, Fortune). Put the three side by side and a buyer's strategy shows. A customer that can threaten to design its own chip gets suppliers to bid with equity. AMD welcomed a very large order and handed over a share of itself to win it, and the risk that came in with the order is a customer that now owns part of its supplier.

Look down the rest of the chart for the bottlenecks. TSMC makes 72.5% of the top ten foundries' revenue. SK hynix holds about half of high-bandwidth memory, though Samsung's share is rising as its next generation ships. ASML is the only company that makes the extreme ultraviolet machines every leading-edge fab needs. Where the chart shows one name at a chokepoint, that company sets terms for everyone above it.

The challengers worth watching change the premise of the row. Cerebras builds one chip from an entire wafer, and went public in May 2026. Substrate is trying to replace the light source in lithography with a particle accelerator that writes with X-rays, and it has money and no revenue. In China, Huawei already ships 28% of AI chips to Nvidia's 34% (IDC, as reported). The orders to watch are the 14A decisions at Intel Foundry in the next two quarters, the first racks of OpenAI's own chips, and the volume ramp of AMD's MI450.

The play in this sector

Every part of this sector is a queue. GE Vernova has gas turbine slots reserved into 2030, with 56 gigawatts of reservations on its books. Dell ended its last fiscal year with a $43 billion backlog of AI servers, and IDC says supply, not demand, is what holds server shipments back. Equinix summed up its quarter with the line that “the vast majority of our largest deals were driven by AI workloads” (Equinix). A queue is a good place to study strategy, because the ones in line are deciding who they are willing to pay to move up.

Money is the clearest example. Meta needed a gigawatt-scale campus in Louisiana and agreed a joint venture in which Blue Owl funds hold 80% and Meta 20%. Meta leases the buildings and provided a guarantee for the first sixteen years (Meta). Meta welcomed that capital because it let the campus be built faster and kept most of the cost off its own balance sheet. The risk that rode in with it is a long guarantee that stays with Meta if demand disappoints. The first horse on the Trojan Horses page describes how private credit enters a bank's relationships in much the same way.

Power gets bought the same way, years ahead. Meta signed three nuclear deals that support up to 6.6 gigawatts and prepaid Oklo, with a first phase targeted for 2030. Amazon's deal with X-energy could reach 5 gigawatts by 2039. A buyer that pays up front to get something built has taken on some of the builder's risk, which is the same arrangement as Renesas's deposit to Wolfspeed (that case).

The networking row holds a quieter shift. Ethernet has moved from under a fifth of AI cluster switch sales to about two-thirds, and Celestica, which builds to the cloud buyers' own designs, led the second quarter ahead of Nvidia. The startup to watch is Ayar Labs, which wants to replace copper links between chips with light. In storage, VAST Data's $30 billion valuation beside DDN's $5 billion shows how far investors will pay for the fastest-growing name in a row with no audited share figure.

The play in this sector

On a September morning Oracle reported that its remaining performance obligations, the contracted revenue it has yet to deliver, had reached $664 billion. A company long known for databases has signed the largest AI contracts in the industry and is paying for the buildings before the revenue arrives. Analysts estimate OpenAI is about half of that backlog, so Oracle has made a very large bet on one customer's ability to keep paying.

Google took the opposite route. Its cloud grew 82% in the June quarter and carries a reported $514 billion backlog, and it sells its own chips and its own models alongside the rental. Amazon still leads on share at 28% and grew AWS 37%, its fastest in eighteen quarters. Microsoft's Azure grew 43% and its commercial backlog is reported at $678 billion. The three are growing at the same time, which is unusual, and it tells you the demand behind them is a shortage of capacity and not a contest for customers.

That shortage built a new tier. CoreWeave rents Nvidia systems to the buyers who cannot build quickly enough, and its customers include Meta, OpenAI and Microsoft, which was about 67% of its 2025 revenue. Nebius holds long contracts from Microsoft and Meta. A landlord for the biggest tenants is a strong business while the shortage lasts, and the interest bills show the cost. CoreWeave paid $640 million of net interest in one quarter.

The practice squad is full of companies selling inference, the serving of finished models to developers. Fireworks AI and Together AI both raised at valuations above $8 billion this summer, and Wasabi's no-exit-fee storage shows a different premise, selling the customer's freedom to leave (that case). The test for the whole sector is conversion. A backlog counts promises, and the quarters ahead show how many of them become revenue.

The play in this sector

Commentators put the selloff at about $285 billion, the fear being that AI agents would do the work that companies paid seats to do. A seat is a license for one human, and an agent is not a human. Vendors answered within months. Salesforce began selling agent actions at about ten cents each, according to commentary, and its Agentforce business reached more than $1.5 billion of annual recurring revenue, up over 240% (reported).

The incumbents' strategy is to own the data and the workflow that agents need to act inside. ServiceNow's subscription revenue grew 24.5% in the June quarter. SAP's cloud backlog grew 27%. Microsoft says it has more than 30 million paid Microsoft 365 Copilot seats, up from 15 million in January (TechCrunch, reported). Each of these companies welcomed the agent because it is a new product line. The risk that came with it is that every agent that finishes a task is one less seat to bill.

The startups sell the outcome and skip the seat. Sierra raised $950 million at a valuation above $15 billion to resolve customer-service issues, and Harvey, Decagon and Glean are doing the same in law, support and search. Meanwhile Databricks closed $5 billion at $190 billion in August, and Palantir is growing at 92.8%, which shows the money is following the platforms where data and models meet.

Private equity is watching the same repricing. Silver Lake is reported in talks to take Workday private at a value above $51 billion, and Thoma Bravo completed Dayforce in February. The question for the second half of the year is whether agent revenue replaces seat revenue fast enough, and Workday's slowing total backlog is the number to keep an eye on.

The play in this sector

Security used to be a market of dozens of products and one overworked buyer. In 2026 the buyers are consolidating the sellers. Google closed its Wiz purchase on 11 March at about $29.5 billion after adjustments (Alphabet). Palo Alto Networks completed CyberArk in February to “Secure the AI Era,” as its release put it. ServiceNow agreed to pay $7.75 billion for Armis and CrowdStrike reportedly paid $740 million for an identity startup.

The thinking behind each purchase is the same. A chief security officer wants fewer vendors and one place to answer for a breach, so the vendor that sells the most modules to the same customer wins the renewal. Palo Alto's next-generation security ARR is $9.10 billion, up 63%, and part of that growth was bought. CrowdStrike sells flexible credits that a customer can spend across modules, and Falcon Flex accounts now exceed $2.29 billion of ARR.

Identity is where the new fight is, because AI agents need identities and permissions as people do. Okta says more than 90% of its customers run agents in production and only 22% are confident the agents are controlled, which is a company-reported figure with an obvious interest behind it. CyberArk now sits inside Palo Alto, and CrowdStrike bought SGNL, so Okta is competing against platforms that treat identity as one module among many.

The Wiz case shows a promise that does real work. Google committed to keep the product available on AWS, Azure and Oracle Cloud, and that is what keeps its value as a neutral layer (the note on that deal). The startups to watch are Island, valued at $6.4 billion, and Cyera, reported at $12 billion, both selling a control point that the platforms do not yet own.

The play in this sector

A chip is not finished when the wafer leaves the fab. It still has to be packaged, and for AI chips that step has become a queue of its own. TSMC does not disclose how much CoWoS packaging capacity it has, and estimates for the end of 2026 run from 115,000 to 140,000 wafers a month. Nvidia is estimated to hold about 60% of it, and Nvidia, Broadcom and AMD together 85% or more (TrendForce, 36Kr). A customer that is third in line for packaging has a reason to sign a ten-year agreement, which is what Amkor is reported to have done with TSMC in June.

Underneath the packaging sits the substrate, the board the chip is mounted on. Three companies make about 75% of the advanced kind, and adding a production line takes eighteen to twenty-four months. The pattern repeats at every layer of this sector. When an input is made by a few specialists and is slow to expand, the buyer who reserves capacity first wins, and everyone else waits. TrendForce expects the packaging squeeze to ease from 2027, which would shift the leverage back toward the chip designers.

Rare earths show the same pattern with a government attached. China's licensing regime for seven medium and heavy rare earths has been in force since April 2025, and the harsher second wave is suspended only until 10 November 2026 (Tech Times). China's exports of rare earths and magnets in January and February were under a third of what they were in the same months of 2024 and 2025. MP Materials produced 840 tonnes of NdPr in the second quarter, up 41%, and is spending most of its capital on magnets. The US defense procurement ban on Chinese magnets starts on 1 January 2027, and one source says US magnet capacity will not be meaningful until 2028.

The thing to watch is the gap between those two dates. A buyer facing a ban in January with domestic supply arriving in 2028 has a bridge to find, and whoever owns the bridge sets the price. The wafers row is thin in this atlas because the research found little current data, and it is marked that way on the cards.

A dated roadmap is a strategy as well as a plan, because it turns every missed step into public evidence. Google's Willow showed errors falling as the machine grew, which does not yet amount to fault tolerance at a useful size.

The thing to watch is whether a claimed advantage survives scrutiny. One third-party tracker says an advantage claimed in July was reproduced classically, which I could not verify with IBM's own materials. PsiQuantum, the practice-squad name, has no 2026 valuation in this research.

The play in this sector

Everything above this sector in the atlas is waiting on it. Siemens Energy reported record orders of EUR 17.9 billion in its third quarter and a record backlog of EUR 162 billion, with grid transformers the largest contributor and data centers the stated reason (Siemens Energy). GE Vernova's backlog was $176 billion. The companies that make heavy electrical equipment are booked years ahead, which gives them pricing power they have not had in a generation.

Batteries and solar are the opposite case, a surplus. CATL holds 39.9% of EV battery installations and BYD's share fell from 17.0% to 14.4% even as its vehicle sales grew. Solar factories are running at about 49% of capacity, and China's competition regulator halted a $7 billion effort to cut polysilicon capacity in January. First Solar, which makes its panels in the United States, has a 45.1 gigawatt backlog worth about $13.6 billion and net sales near $1.06 billion in the quarter. A surplus abroad and a tariff wall at home are what make that backlog possible.

Tesla's storage business deployed 13.7 gigawatt-hours in the third quarter, up 9.6% and under what analysts expected, and its energy gross margin rose from 20.4% to 33.1%, helped by tariff refunds and warranty reimbursements that the company named. A margin that rises for one-off reasons is worth reading carefully.

The practice squad is the new power. Fervo, a geothermal developer, priced its IPO at $27 on 13 May and raised about $1.89 billion, with one analysis putting its 2025 revenue near $138,000. Helion reportedly raised $465 million at a $15.5 billion valuation and has a Microsoft deal for 50 megawatts around 2028. Commonwealth Fusion has between $2.9 and $3.9 billion and expects its SPARC machine to switch on around the turn of the year. The valuations price a premise, and the first plant that delivers power will show which of them was right.

Method, and what this does not cover

Every figure on a card carries a period and a link. Sourced means a company filing or release, or a named research firm such as Dell'Oro, IDC, TrendForce, Counterpoint, Synergy or Nielsen. Reported means credible press or an industry tracker without the underlying document in hand. Inferred means the line or the badge is my reading of the structure and no source states it. The lines that say why a company leads and what could change the order are my reading of the evidence on each card.

A starter is a company with a measurable lead in its row. A second-string player has shipping product or named customers. A practice-squad player is private or recently public, with a disclosed funding round or a named transaction. Where sources conflict on a figure, the card shows the range or says that the sources conflict, and a startup whose funding could not be confirmed was left off. Search summaries can paraphrase, so the figures above come from the cited pages and not from memory, and some pages could not be opened directly. Those are marked reported.

Not covered. The atlas has 17 sectors, and some industries are not in it, including the software that runs logistics and retail supply chains, education, media production outside streaming, and real-estate technology. The companies in the atlas are mostly American and Chinese, and Europe, India, Japan and Korea are thin outside the sectors where they lead.
