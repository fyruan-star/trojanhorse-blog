TheBuyer’s Bank

FIVE VERTICALS, FIFTY-SEVEN COMPANIES
AND THE ONE CHAIR NOBODY WAS SITTING IN

Francis Ruan

In July 2024 a four-year-old Israeli security company walked away from twenty-three billion dollars. Wiz had just crossed $500m of annual recurring revenue, up from $350m the year before and $100m in the summer of 2022, and its chief executive told staff the plan was an IPO and a billion in ARR. The stated concern was antitrust. Google was already defending two Justice Department suits, and nobody wanted to spend a year and a half inside a review that might end in nothing at all.

Eight months later Wiz sold to Google anyway. Thirty-two billion dollars. Same buyer, same antitrust problem, thirty-nine percent more money.

Somebody sat in that room and closed the gap, and on Google’s side it was BofA Securities, exclusively. That is the best technology mandate any bank won in the period. I have spent a while inside the record of everything else BofA advised on across 2025 and 2026, and what interests me here is that Wiz is not the exception in that record. It is the cleanest example of the rule. My read is that BofA in technology is the buyer’s bank, and that this is a strategy rather than an accident.

First, what the record actually is

There is no public ledger of what a coverage desk did last year. No filing names an office. A proxy tells you BofA delivered the opinion and roughly what it was paid; it never tells you whether Palo Alto, New York or London did the work. So I built the closest thing available: every SEC filing from January 2025 through September 2026 naming BofA Securities as financial adviser or carrying its fairness opinion. Fifty-seven distinct companies.

Twelve of them are real estate and REITs. Nine are transport and business services. Eight are healthcare. Four are utilities. Eighteen are industrial, consumer, energy and financial. Six carry technology SIC codes.

The largest single item is Union Pacific and Norfolk Southern at $84.4bn, with BofA on the target. That is a railroad. This is a real assets and utilities house with a very good research desk attached, and the six technology names are worth walking through one at a time, because each one is a different vertical with a different logic and the same seating chart.

Security, and what Google was solving for

Google Cloud has been a durable third behind AWS and Azure for years, and third place in infrastructure is a specific kind of problem. You cannot win it on price and you rarely win it on features. You win it when something forces an enterprise to reconsider a decision it made eight years ago, and in practice the thing that forces that reconsideration is security.

Here is the part I find genuinely clever. Wiz is deliberately multicloud, and Google committed publicly to keeping it working on AWS, Azure and Oracle. Read that as strategy rather than good manners. Google did not buy a feature for its own cloud. It bought the security layer sitting above everybody’s cloud, which means it now gets paid on the Amazon estate it was never going to win, and it holds the relationship with the security team, which is the team that gets consulted when workloads move.

And the price tells you who had leverage. Wiz walked in 2024 because the alternative was real: half a billion of recurring revenue growing at that slope is an IPO, not a distressed sale. The $9bn Google added was not a revaluation of the asset over eight months. My read is that it was the cost of removing an alternative, plus whatever deal protections made the antitrust wait survivable for a seller who had already said no once.

Goldman sat with Wiz. BofA sat with Google.

Outsourcing, and two ways to leave the tape

Both other technology names in 2025 were business process outsourcing, which I do not think is coincidence.

Consider what it is like to run a seat-based outsourcing company while language models get cheaper every quarter. Your revenue is priced per agent per hour. Your customers are reading the same research you are. Even if the disruption takes a decade, the multiple compresses now, because the market prices the narrative long before it prices the cash flow. I want to be careful here: I have no evidence that either company’s revenue was impaired. My read is simply that the repricing risk was obvious to everyone in the room, and that if you believe a hard rebuild is coming, you would rather not do it with a quarterly scoreboard over your shoulder.

In May 2025 TaskUs went private at $16.50 a share, roughly $1.62bn, with Blackstone alongside the two co-founders who built it. BofA advised the buyer group. Evercore sat with the special committee, which is the seat that exists specifically to protect everyone else from the founders on the other side of the table.

By July, TELUS had made a run at the piece of TELUS Digital it did not already own, and the special committee retained BofA as independent adviser with BMO as valuator. The parent took it out at $4.50 a share, about $539m of consideration. Same diagnosis, different route: one company bought its freedom with sponsor money, the other was simply pulled back inside a parent.

Note which mandate was larger. BofA was on the buying end of the $1.62bn deal and the independent end of the $539m one.

Data, and who owns the join

Then 2026, and a vertical that is really one idea wearing two outfits.

Advertising has spent a decade losing the ability to follow a person around. What replaced surveillance is matching: you hold records, the advertiser holds records, and somebody has to decide those two records are the same human being without either side handing over the name. That capability is called identity resolution and LiveRamp is the biggest independent one.

In May 2026 Publicis agreed to buy it at $38.50 a share, $2.167bn of enterprise value. Publicis framed it as data co-creation for smarter agents, which is the 2026 way of saying that autonomous ad buying is useless without a trustworthy join. My read is more prosaic and more interesting. An agency that rents the matching layer has a competitor’s hand on its own cost line, forever, and gets squeezed precisely when the layer becomes essential. Publicis bought the hand off its throat. BofA advised Publicis.

Three months later CoStar closed an $800m purchase of Zonda, consolidating new-home data into a graph it already dominates for commercial and residential. Same logic again: in a data business the second-best set is worth almost nothing, so the only sensible position is to own the whole thing. BofA advised CoStar. The buyer, again.

Travel, and a poison pill that lasted four days

The Sabre mandate is the most instructive of the six because you can watch the clock.

Between April and November 2025, Constellation Software built a 9.7% economic position in Sabre, 4.7% in common stock and another 5% through derivatives. That structure is itself a message: derivatives let you accumulate quietly and past the point where common stock alone would force disclosure. By February 2026 Constellation held 50,157,523 shares outright, 12.7% of the company.

Understand who is doing this. Constellation buys vertical market software and does not sell it, ever. They are the most patient acquirer in the industry and they are famously indifferent to being liked. Sabre, meanwhile, was carrying real leverage and trading at a price that reflected it.

There were weeks of governance discussions. On 26 February, Constellation abruptly ended them. On 1 March, Sabre’s board adopted a limited-duration rights plan with a 15% trigger and a one-year term running to February 2027, with BofA Securities as financial adviser and Kirkland as counsel. On 5 March, Sabre gave Constellation a board seat, Constellation agreed to stand still at 15%, and the plan’s expiry was accelerated.

Four days. Which is the point, and I think it is widely misunderstood. A pill is not a wall. It is a clock you start when the other side stops returning calls, and it worked exactly as designed: it converted an accumulation into a negotiation and put a ceiling on the stake. Then in September BofA turned up again as dealer manager on Sabre’s secured debt tenders, which is the same vulnerability approached from the other end. The leverage was why the price was cheap, and the price was why Constellation came.

Which is the pattern, and the pattern is the product

Line up all six. Google buying. Blackstone buying. Publicis buying. CoStar buying. A special committee on a $539m squeeze-out. A board defending itself, and then refinancing.

Not one of them is a founder deciding to sell.

So here is the value proposition as the record describes it, rather than as a pitchbook would. A desk that is genuinely excellent at representing people deploying capital, with a universal bank’s balance sheet behind the advice. Acquirers need bridges, revolvers, leveraged finance and a dealer manager. Qatalyst and Centerview cannot offer any of that at any price. That is a real product and BofA sells it at the highest level in the world, which the Wiz mandate proves at maximum difficulty.

What it does not appear to sell is the seat next to a founder in the week they decide.

Why I think that gap costs more than it looks

I want to steelman the position first, because there is a good argument for it. Covering founders is expensive and it does not amortise until they exit, which may be never. Acquirer relationships renew on a schedule, attach to an institution rather than a person, and survive banker turnover. If you are running a balance sheet the size of BofA’s, the buy side is the rational business. A forty-person boutique can live on sell-side lightning. A universal bank cannot.

Here is the asymmetry that argument misses, and it is specific to this industry.

A buy-side mandate is a procurement decision made by a corporate development team, and it ends at closing. A sell-side mandate attaches to a human being at the most consequential moment of their working life. That person then founds something else, joins three boards, writes angel cheques and eventually partner cheques. Sell a railroad and the seller retires. Sell a company at thirty-two and you are a repeat player for four decades, and every one of those decades has a banker attached to it.

The buy-side relationship closes with the deal. The sell-side one follows somebody around Northern California for thirty years.

The strongest argument against me

My instrument is imperfect and I would rather break it myself. EDGAR only sees transactions with a US public filer, and Silicon Valley’s deal count is overwhelmingly private targets that generate nothing. Fairness opinions are mostly target-side, so buy-side work is structurally undercounted. Wiz is both failures in one example, which is why it is in this piece at all.

Two things survive it. The undercount runs toward missing sell-side public deals, and BofA’s public sell-side work is not missing, it is abundant. It is just abundant in REITs. And the large technology sales that did surface went elsewhere with some consistency: Palo Alto Networks used JPMorgan on the roughly $25bn CyberArk deal while CyberArk used Qatalyst, and Confluent used Morgan Stanley selling to IBM at $11.9bn.

My concern is not that BofA had a bad year. It was top five in the Americas and it advised the target on an $84.4bn rail merger. My concern is narrower. A franchise can be top five by value and still not be in the room where the decision gets made.

Which is what March 2026 was about

In March 2026 Reuters reported four senior technology hires, sourced to internal memos. Gary Kirkham from Centerview as executive vice chair, a man who had already spent over twenty years at BofA as global co-head of TMT. Jason Rowe from Goldman Sachs as global co-head of technology investment banking, one of three software co-heads there across two decades. Mahir Zaimoglu and Patrik Czornik from JPMorgan, both London. It followed Kevin Brunner leaving for JPMorgan as global chairman of investment banking and M&A, and Ric Spencer leaving for Citigroup.

You do not hire a Goldman software co-head for his execution. Execution is the thing BofA has demonstrably got. You hire him for his phone, and specifically for twenty years of founders in it who will take his call in the week they are deciding.

Which is the bank agreeing with the diagnosis. The hires are the admission.

What I would watch

  • Whether a venture-backed sell-side mandate appears in the next four quarters. That is the only evidence that will show the hires worked.
  • Whether the balance sheet keeps winning sponsor take-privates, because that is the part of the franchise that already functions and gets almost no credit.
  • Whether Rowe’s first mandates are Google-shaped or founder-shaped.
  • Whether senior seats actually land in Northern California, given that three of the four announced hires sit in London or New York.

That last one is the tell I would trust most. A bank that intends to win founder mandates in the Bay Area eventually has to put senior people in the Bay Area, and so far the announced geography says otherwise.

Wiz said no at twenty-three billion and yes at thirty-two, and the bank that closed that gap was sitting on the side that pays. Everything else in the record says the same thing.
