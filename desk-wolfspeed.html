Wolfspeed and the$2bn Deposit

A customer's prepayment is a loan without a lender's protections

Francis Ruan

In July 2023 the Japanese chipmaker Renesas gave Wolfspeed $2 billion in advance, under a ten-year agreement for silicon carbide wafers. By June 2025 Wolfspeed was in Chapter 11 and Renesas was converting the deposit, by then $2.062 billion, into a stake in the company. I think a prepayment of that size is a loan, and that Renesas carried a lender's risk without a lender's protections.

How the arrangement was described

The 2023 agreement was presented as securing supply. Renesas needed guaranteed wafers for its own power chips, and Wolfspeed needed cash to build factories before demand arrived. The deposit solved both problems, and Renesas described it as support for Wolfspeed's capacity expansion (Wolfspeed). On that reading the deposit was a purchase order paid early.

A change of material

For sixty years power electronics improved silicon, with smaller transistors, cleaner processes and better packaging. Silicon carbide changed the material instead of refining the old one. Its physics supports higher voltages and temperatures in the same space, which opened a different product, an electric car that goes further on the same battery. Musk's approach to the car is the comparison. Do not ask how to make the combustion engine better. Ask what the vehicle looks like if the engine is not the starting point.

The reframing has a cost that shows up in this case. A new material does not only replace a part, it changes who has to spend first. Silicon carbide needed new factories before the cars that used it were selling in volume, so the customers who depended on it ended up pre-funding the capacity. Renesas's deposit was a customer paying for the new supply to exist, and when the timing slipped the customer took the loss.

Why it was closer to a loan

A deposit of $2 billion, against a supplier that was spending heavily and borrowing, has the economics of a loan. The money went to the supplier up front, repayment depended on the supplier's later performance, and the customer's remedy if the supplier failed was a claim in bankruptcy. A lender would normally have taken security, set covenants and ranked ahead of the supplier's owners. A customer holding a deposit usually has none of those.

The test came when demand slowed. Electric vehicle growth eased, Wolfspeed's debt grew and in May 2025 the company warned of going-concern doubt and said it might restructure in court. Renesas then negotiated a Restructuring Support Agreement with Wolfspeed and its principal creditors in June 2025.

What the conversion shows

Under the agreement Renesas converted the $2.062 billion deposit into $204 million of convertible notes due 2031, common stock equal to 38.7 percent of Wolfspeed's shares at completion, which is 17.9 percent fully diluted before warrants, and warrants for 5 percent more on a fully diluted basis. Renesas said it expected a loss of roughly 250 billion yen on a deposit of about 301 billion yen, by its own estimate (Renesas announcement). That implies recovering about seventeen cents on the dollar.

The plan became effective on 29 September 2025, with one regulatory condition outstanding. On 30 January 2026 Wolfspeed announced that the final clearance had been received and that it had issued about 16.9 million shares to Renesas, in a company of roughly 45 million shares. Renesas took a board seat (Wolfspeed). The largest customer is now also a shareholder, which aligns its interests with the supplier's and also ties more of its risk to it.

What the companies do

Silicon carbide is a compound semiconductor. Power chips made from it switch higher voltages at higher temperatures and waste less energy as heat than silicon chips, which matters in an electric vehicle inverter, where battery power is converted to motor power, and in solar and grid equipment. The material is difficult to make. The crystals grow slowly in furnaces above 2,000 degrees Celsius and the finished wafers are close to diamond in hardness. Wolfspeed, formerly Cree, grew these crystals and built the Mohawk Valley fab in New York, which it describes as the first 200mm silicon carbide fab. Renesas turns the wafers into power chips.

The case also shows why a customer would take the risk. A new material needs factories before the products that use it are selling in volume, so whoever depends on the supply may have to help pay for the capacity. Silicon carbide was a change in what power chips are made of and not an improvement to silicon, and that change moved the cost of building forward onto the customers.

Competition

From my own reading, in silicon carbide Wolfspeed competes with Infineon, STMicroelectronics, onsemi, ROHM, Coherent in wafers and a fast-growing group of Chinese suppliers that are pushing prices down. Renesas competes in power and automotive chips with Infineon, STMicroelectronics, NXP and Texas Instruments. Wolfspeed's survival after the filing depends on whether a 200mm fab can run at enough of its capacity to cover its costs, because vehicle demand swings quickly and a plant running below capacity loses money.

The strongest objection

The objection is that a prepayment secured something a loan could not, which was the supply itself. Renesas received wafers it could not otherwise get during the period when they were scarce, and a loss on the deposit does not mean the arrangement was unwise.

That depends on how many wafers were delivered against the deposit, which the announcements do not state. The evidence that would decide the question is the quantity and price of wafers Renesas received relative to the cost of buying them in the open market. If it received substantial supply at a discount, the loss was partly the price of that supply.

What a reader should take from it

When a customer prepays a supplier, treat the deposit as credit exposure and ask what protection the customer holds. The announcements name no advisers on this transaction. A BofA Securities banker lists it among their work, and what a bank does in a conversion of this kind is to work out what the claim is worth and which instruments can hold that value after the company emerges.
