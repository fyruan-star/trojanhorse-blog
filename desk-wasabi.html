Wasabi and theExit Fee

A STORAGE VENDOR THAT COMPETED ON THE METER
RATHER THAN THE PRICE PER TERABYTE

Francis Ruan

In January 2026 Wasabi Technologies announced a $70 million equity round led by L2 Point Management, with Pure Storage and existing investors including Fidelity taking part. The round valued the company at about $1.8 billion and brought its total funding past $600 million.

The bill

Every company that stores data in the cloud eventually meets the same surprise. Putting data in is cheap. Taking it out is not. Cloud providers charge for egress, which is the cost of moving your own data out of their system, and they also charge for the requests you make along the way. Storage looks cheap until you try to use it or leave.

David Friend and Jeff Flowers knew that bill well. They had co-founded Carbonite, the online backup company, and paid cloud storage fees for years. In 2015 they started Wasabi on a simple claim, which was that the pricing model is the problem.

What the bank did

A banker on BofA Securities’ Palo Alto software team lists a $70 million Series D1 for Wasabi among their transactions. I could not find that label in public coverage, which calls the January 2026 round an equity round. Foley & Lardner is named as Wasabi’s legal adviser and I found no public source naming BofA, so what follows describes the typical work.

A follow-on round for a private infrastructure company is a positioning exercise. By then Wasabi had a record, including a $250 million package in 2022 made of about $125 million of Series D equity and a debt facility, which Barclays placed. The adviser’s task is to answer one question for investors, which is what is different now. In 2026 the answer was AI. Data lakes, training sets and model checkpoints all need somewhere cheap and always available to live, and nobody wants an exit fee on a petabyte.

The adviser also shapes who is in the room. L2 Point led this round as it did in 2022. A strategic investor like Pure Storage tells the market that this is not only a financial bet.

Why $1.8 billion

I could not find Wasabi’s revenue in the sources I checked, so I will not offer a multiple. The case for the price is structural.

Storage is sticky, because data does not move casually, so revenue compounds. The pricing is flat and simple, which shortens sales cycles. AI workloads are raising demand for cheap, always-available data. A strategic investor in the round gives the valuation a second source of support beyond the financial funds.

What it does, twice

In technical terms, Wasabi sells S3-compatible object storage, which means software written for Amazon’s storage works with Wasabi unchanged. It is hot storage. Every object is available at the same speed, with no cold archive tier to wait on. Pricing is a flat rate per terabyte per month with no egress fees and no per-request fees.

In plain terms, imagine a storage unit that charges only for the space and never for opening the door or taking your things out. Every other unit charges each time you open it.

However

However, the industry spent a decade competing on the wrong number.

Amazon, Microsoft and Google each tried to win storage by lowering the price per gigabyte and then adding tiers, discounts and special classes to optimize the bill. Every improvement worked inside the same model, which was cheap to store and costly to move. It was a faster horse.

The Musk move is to scratch the premise. Cars did not win by being a better horse. Wasabi did not try to be a cheaper S3. It asked what storage looks like if the meter on leaving does not exist. Remove egress fees and the product becomes something else, a place data can live without a toll, and a promise the incumbents’ own business models make hard to match.

That is the Trojan Horse in this deal. It looks like a cheaper storage vendor. It is actually an attack on the way the incumbents make money, because the egress fee is a lock-in tool as much as a revenue line. Wasabi walked it in as a pricing difference, and the real change was to the customer’s freedom to leave.

Whether it holds

The hardest test is that a horse can be copied. Google announced in January 2024 that it would waive data transfer fees for customers leaving its cloud, AWS followed in March, and Microsoft after that. Reporting links all three moves to new European Union rules. The waivers come with conditions, since they apply to customers who leave entirely and not to those who stay, so the advantage is narrower and not gone. What remains is the price, the simplicity and the focus. Those are real, but they are less structural than a gap in how the business model works.

In its favor are predictable pricing that buyers like, S3 compatibility that makes switching in easy, a strategic backer in Pure Storage and demand from AI. Against it, the hyperscalers can cut prices and have far deeper pockets, storage is a scale business that favors the giants, and pure-play storage vendors have historically earned thin margins.

Who it fights

From my own reading, AWS S3, Microsoft Azure Blob Storage and Google Cloud Storage, along with Backblaze B2, Cloudflare R2 and the object-storage lines of Dell, NetApp and Pure Storage.

What the record does not show

BofA’s role, the Series D1 label and Wasabi’s revenue are all absent from the public sources I found. The company is reported to have secured a $250 million credit facility in April 2026, which I have not tied to this round.

Competing on a rival’s pricing model works until the rival changes it, and then what is left is everything else.
