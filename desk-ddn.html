DDN takes $300m fromBlackstone

A founder-owned company sold about six percent of itself

Francis Ruan

For twenty-six years two founders owned DDN outright. Alex Bouzari and Paul Bloch started the storage company in 1998 and built it into a profitable business without outside money. In January 2025 Blackstone Tactical Opportunities invested $300 million at a $5 billion valuation, the first outside capital DDN had accepted. I think a deal of this kind should be read as a decision about price and control and not mainly as a decision about funding, and that BofA Securities, which was reported to be DDN's exclusive adviser, was solving a problem the founders had not faced before.

The usual reading

A capital raise is normally read as a company getting money it needs to grow. DDN's announcement framed it that way. The release said the investment would “help fund DDN's continued rapid growth,” and Bouzari said that “Blackstone's support accelerates our mission to redefine the enterprise AI infrastructure category” (Pulse 2.0, reproducing the release).

That reading is incomplete for this company. DDN had been profitable for decades and had never needed outside capital, and $300 million at a $5 billion valuation is at most about six percent of the company. The founders sold a small slice and kept control.

What the bank was reported to do

BofA Securities was named in the announcement as DDN's exclusive financial adviser, and the release names no adviser for Blackstone. The public record shows nothing further, so what follows is what the assignment involves in general terms and not a description of this one.

A founder-owned company that has never sold equity has no price to anchor a negotiation. The adviser's work is to establish one, by turning twenty-six years of quiet, profitable growth into a case an investor can underwrite in a few weeks, deciding which investors to approach, and negotiating what the founders keep. A strategic buyer would want control, and the founders did not want to sell. A growth fund might want a board seat and a ten-year hold. A group that can write a large check, accept a minority position and move quickly was a workable match, and Blackstone's Jas Khaira said, “We are thrilled to be the first institutional investor in DDN.”

Why $5 billion

The announcement gave no revenue figure. Forbes has reported revenue of roughly $400 million in 2024, about $500 million in 2025 and a projection near $1 billion for 2026 (AI Weekly), figures that are not audited. On those numbers $5 billion is about ten times 2025 revenue and five times the 2026 projection, which is a premium price for a business growing that quickly and an unusual one for this part of the market, where most companies selling into AI infrastructure spend more than they earn.

The rest of the price reflects proximity to the scarce input. DDN says its systems support more than 500,000 NVIDIA GPUs, including xAI's Colossus cluster. That is a company claim and not a measured market share. The economics behind the price are in the note on this deal in Ten Years. In short, a storage supplier whose product keeps very expensive processors working can be priced against the cost of those processors.

What I infer

The investment's main product may be the price itself. A $5 billion valuation set by a major investor gives DDN a reference point for hiring, for negotiating with customers and for any later financing or listing. I infer this from the structure, since a company that needed no money took a small amount at a high price, and no source states it as the purpose.

The strongest objection

The objection is that the founders did want the money. Building a new product platform called Infinia and supplying clusters of this size is capital intensive, and a profitable company can still choose to raise capital to grow faster. The stated use of proceeds supports that.

The two readings predict different behavior. If the capital was needed, DDN should raise more or deploy it visibly in capacity and product within a year or two. If the price was the aim, the next evidence would be a further financing or a listing at a higher valuation, with the $300 million largely unspent.

Competition and durability

From my own reading and not from the announcement, DDN's competitors include VAST Data, the most aggressive private rival, along with WEKA, Pure Storage, NetApp, Dell's PowerScale line, IBM Storage Scale, HPE's Cray storage line and Hammerspace. DDN's edge is a long record inside the largest and most demanding clusters, where a storage failure costs weeks of training and buyers do not switch vendors lightly.

Three risks bear on whether the valuation holds. A few very large AI buyers drive much of the growth, so customer concentration is high. The GPU spending cycle can slow, and storage orders slow with it. And hyperscalers or NVIDIA's own reference designs could pull storage in-house or toward a short list of approved vendors. DDN's decades of profit mean it can fund its own product cycle through a slowdown, which most of its younger rivals cannot.

What a reader should take from it

When a founder-owned company takes a small minority investment at a high valuation, ask what the investment gives the company other than money. The adviser's role in establishing the price is part of what was bought.
