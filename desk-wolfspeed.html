Wolfspeed and the$2bn Deposit

WHAT HAPPENS WHEN A CUSTOMER PREPAYS A SUPPLIER
AND THE SUPPLIER CANNOT DELIVER THE FUTURE

Francis Ruan

In July 2023 the Japanese chipmaker Renesas gave a supplier $2 billion in advance. The money was a deposit tied to a ten-year agreement for silicon carbide wafers, and it later grew to $2.062 billion. By June 2025 the supplier, Wolfspeed, was in Chapter 11, and Renesas was negotiating to turn the deposit into a piece of the company.

The prepayment

The logic was clean. Wolfspeed needed cash to build factories before demand arrived. Renesas needed guaranteed wafers for its own power chips. A deposit solved both problems at once. It was a loan dressed as a purchase order.

Then the demand curve bent. Electric vehicle growth slowed and Wolfspeed’s debt piled up. In May 2025 the company warned of going-concern doubt and said it might restructure in court. Renesas’s deposit was suddenly the largest claim on a company that might not survive.

What the bank did

A banker on BofA Securities’ Palo Alto software team lists this transaction among their work. Renesas’s announcement names no advisers, so what follows describes this kind of assignment and not the record of this one.

A customer deposit inside a bankruptcy is an awkward thing. It is not a bond, not a loan and not equity. Is it a creditor claim, a prepayment for goods never delivered or something else? An adviser’s first job is to work out what the claim is worth, who stands ahead of and behind it, and which instruments could hold its value after the company comes out the other side.

Renesas negotiated a Restructuring Support Agreement with Wolfspeed and its principal creditors in June 2025. The deposit would convert into three things. The first was $204 million of convertible notes due 2031. The second was common stock equal to 38.7 percent of Wolfspeed’s shares at completion, or 17.9 percent fully diluted before warrants. The third was warrants for another 5 percent fully diluted. If regulatory approvals ran late, Renesas would hold instruments of equal economic value until they arrived.

Why this price

There was no price tag here in the usual sense. The valuation was a haircut.

Renesas said it expected a loss of roughly 250 billion yen on a deposit of about 301 billion yen, by its own internal estimate. That implies recovering about seventeen cents on the dollar through the new instruments. A number that stark is the market’s verdict on a customer-funded bet that went wrong. The real question was never what Wolfspeed was worth. It was how much Renesas could take without destroying a supplier it still needed.

What the companies do, twice

In technical terms, silicon carbide is a compound semiconductor. Power chips made from it switch higher voltages at higher temperatures and waste much less energy as heat than silicon chips do. That matters in an electric vehicle inverter, which turns battery power into motor power, and in solar and grid equipment. The material is hard to make. The crystals grow slowly in furnaces above 2,000 degrees Celsius and the finished wafers are close to diamond in hardness. Wolfspeed, formerly Cree, grew these crystals and built the Mohawk Valley fab in New York, which it describes as the first 200mm silicon carbide fab.

In plain terms, power chips are the valves that control electricity. Silicon valves leak some energy as heat and silicon carbide valves leak far less. Wolfspeed makes the plate the valves are cut from. Renesas turns the plates into valves.

However

However, this story starts with a premise somebody scratched.

For sixty years power electronics improved silicon, with smaller transistors, cleaner processes and better packaging. That was a faster horse. Silicon carbide asked a different question. Instead of shrinking the same material, change the material. The physics of the new one supports higher voltages and temperatures in the same space, and that opened a different product, an electric car that goes further on the same battery.

Elon Musk’s approach to the car is the comparison. Do not ask how to make the combustion engine better. Ask what the vehicle looks like if the engine is not the starting point. Silicon carbide is that logic applied to the chip inside the car.

The same idea has a cost. A new material does not only replace a part. It changes who has to spend first. Silicon carbide needed whole new factories before the cars that used it were selling in volume, so the people building the future ended up pre-funding it. Renesas’s deposit was a customer paying for the new horse to be born. When the timing slipped, the customer took the loss.

Whether it holds

The plan of reorganization became effective on 29 September 2025. One condition remained, which was regulatory clearance. On 30 January 2026 Wolfspeed announced the final step, the issue of about 16.9 million shares to Renesas in a company of roughly 45 million shares, and Renesas took a board seat. The largest customer is now also a shareholder, and that aligns the two of them.

Long-term demand for silicon carbide is not really in doubt. Electric vehicles, charging and grid equipment need it, and a 200mm fab is a scarce asset. The risks are those that caused the filing in the first place. Vehicle demand swings quickly and capacity was built ahead of it. Chinese wafer makers are pushing prices down. A fab running below capacity loses money.

Who they fight

From my own reading, in silicon carbide the competitors are Infineon, STMicroelectronics, onsemi, ROHM, Coherent in wafers and a fast-growing group of Chinese suppliers. Renesas competes in power and automotive chips with Infineon, STMicroelectronics, NXP and Texas Instruments.

What the record does not show

I found no public source tying BofA to a particular side of this transaction. The profile abbreviates the deposit as a CRD and I treated that as the customer deposit without confirming the term. The loss figure is Renesas’s own estimate from June 2025 and the final accounting may differ.

A customer who prepays a supplier has become a lender without the protections a lender would have asked for.
