The strategy and incentives behind investment banking

How Banks Win the Mandate

Why a company chooses one adviser over another.

Imagine a software company agrees to buy a competitor for $5 billion. The announcement explains the price, the strategic rationale, and the expected benefits. Near the bottom, it names the investment banks that advised each side.

Those names deserve some attention. Each bank had to persuade a company to trust it with a decision that could change the company’s future. Understanding how it won that trust tells you something about the transaction itself.

That is the idea behind The Trojan Horse. I want to make the strategy inside investment banking understandable to people outside the industry. There are interesting problems here: how companies negotiate, how advisers build an advantage, and how incentives influence decisions involving billions of dollars. You should be able to understand those problems without having worked at a bank.

A useful place to begin is the mandate, which is simply the assignment to advise a client.

Before a bank can help a company buy or sell a business, it has to win that assignment. That competition explains a surprising amount about how investment banking works.

The company is buying judgment

Return to the software acquisition. The buyer needs to decide what the competitor is worth, how much it can afford to pay, and whether owning the business would improve its position. It also needs to consider the seller’s alternatives. A rival bidder could change the negotiation. So could the seller’s willingness to remain independent.

Financial analysis helps answer these questions. The difficult part is deciding which assumptions deserve confidence and how to act while important facts remain uncertain.

On a $5 billion purchase, a 1 percent difference in price is $50 million. A useful adviser might help the buyer avoid paying that extra amount. It might also recognize that the business deserves a higher price, or that the buyer should stop negotiating altogether.

This makes choosing an adviser difficult. The client is paying for judgment whose quality will become clearer only after decisions have been made. A polished presentation can demonstrate preparation. It offers less certainty about how someone will behave when a negotiation becomes difficult.

Relationships help address that problem.

Banks organize teams around industries so their people can develop expertise and stay close to potential clients. A banker covering software can study competitors, follow management changes, and bring executives ideas before there is a transaction to discuss.

Consider two bankers approaching the same chief executive. One arrives when a sale becomes likely. The other has spent years offering useful observations about the business, including reasons to reject previous acquisition ideas. The second banker has given the executive more evidence on which to judge the advice.

A formal pitch can still matter. The relationship influences how the client evaluates what it hears.

Financing changes the choice

Now suppose our buyer needs to borrow $2 billion to complete the acquisition.

The adviser selection becomes more complicated. The company needs advice about the purchase and confidence that it can fund it.

A large bank can offer several services through different teams, including acquisition advice, lending, and help raising money from investors. Bank of America describes this combination explicitly in its corporate and investment banking business.

For the buyer, that combination can reduce coordination problems. A recommendation becomes more useful when the company understands how it could pay for it.

There is historical evidence that lending relationships influence adviser selection. A 2002 New York Fed study by Linda Allen, Julapa Jagtiani, Stavros Peristiani, and Anthony Saunders found that acquirers tended to hire banks that already lent to them. The authors linked that choice to access to acquisition financing. Their findings describe a historical sample, but they help explain the commercial logic of combining lending and advice.

My interpretation is straightforward: financing capability gives a bank another reason for the client to choose it. The size of that advantage depends on what the transaction requires.

The same advantage creates a tension

Suppose a bank could earn money from advising our software buyer and arranging its debt. Completing the acquisition could produce revenue from both activities.

The client wants the adviser to recommend whatever creates the most value, including walking away. The bank’s potential revenues introduce another consideration. The New York Fed researchers identified conflicts associated with advising and lending to an acquirer, including the bank’s existing and prospective credit exposure.

This helps explain the appeal of independent advisory firms.

Evercore makes the point in its own annual report, which says the firm was founded on the belief that there was an opportunity free of the potential conflicts of interest created within large, multi-product capital intensive financial institutions. Centerview describes itself more simply as an independent investment bank providing objective advice. Neither firm lends, so neither has a loan to protect.

Independence still leaves other incentives to consider. Advisory fees can depend heavily on completing a transaction. The same Evercore filing states that the firm earns a majority of its revenue from advisory engagements and is not paid until the successful consummation of the transactions.

The useful question for a client is how an adviser gets paid and how that could influence its recommendations.

The client can combine advantages

Our software buyer could hire an independent firm for acquisition advice and use a large bank for financing. It could also appoint multiple advisers with different strengths.

This is why a simple division between large banks advising buyers and independent firms advising sellers misses too much. Buyers need independent judgment. Sellers may value a large bank’s industry knowledge and relationships. A particular banker’s experience can matter more than the institution’s general business model.

The strategic problem for each bank is to identify where its strengths matter enough to win the assignment.

A lender may have an advantage when financing is difficult. An industry specialist may have an advantage when understanding the asset requires unusual expertise. A trusted adviser may have an advantage when a board faces a decision it has never encountered before.

The strongest proposition depends on the client’s problem.

Reputation changes the incentives

There is a game theory problem here that I find especially interesting.

An adviser may benefit financially from getting a transaction completed today. It also wants the client’s next assignment and recommendations to other executives. Those future opportunities give it a reason to protect the quality of its advice.

A recommendation to abandon a deal can therefore have commercial value. The adviser gives up a potential fee while giving the client evidence that its judgment can be trusted.

That outcome depends on the value placed on future business. Reputation creates an incentive for good advice, but clients still need to evaluate each recommendation.

It also helps explain why senior bankers matter so much to their firms. Their expertise and client relationships influence future revenue. Evercore lists the departure of senior professionals as a risk, warning that it could jeopardize relationships with clients and result in the loss of client engagements and revenues.

Read the adviser list differently

The next time you read an acquisition announcement, look at the advisers alongside the purchase price. Consider what the buyer needed to finance, what expertise the transaction required, and which relationships might have influenced the choice. Merger filings and financing announcements can help you investigate those possibilities.

You will begin to see each bank as a business competing to solve a particular problem.

That is what I want The Trojan Horse to help you understand: how the people involved assess their options, respond to incentives, and earn the right to influence a consequential decision.
