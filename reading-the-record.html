Reading theRecord

IN THE QUIETEST YEAR FOR DEALS SINCE 2009,
CISCO PAID $28BN FOR LOG FILES

Francis Ruan

Cisco agreed to buy Splunk in September 2023 for $157 a share in cash, about $28 billion. It was the largest acquisition Cisco had ever made, announced into the quietest year for technology dealmaking since the financial crisis, for a company whose product reads log files.

What a log file is

Every piece of software writes down what it just did. A user signed in here. This request failed. This server restarted at 4.12am. These notes are called logs and a large organisation produces an almost unimaginable quantity of them, nearly all of which nobody will ever read.

Splunk collects all of it and makes it searchable. That is the product. It is the least glamorous sentence on this page.

Why that is worth twenty-eight billion dollars

Two reasons, and the second is the interesting one.

The first is security. When somebody breaks into a company, the evidence is in the logs, scattered across thousands of systems. Finding it means searching all of them at once. This category is called SIEM, security information and event management, and Splunk largely defined it.

The second is that modern software is assembled from hundreds of small services written by different teams, and when something breaks, no single person understands the whole thing any more. The only way to know what happened is to read the record. The industry calls this observability, which is a long word for knowing what your own systems are doing.

Here is what was in the horse

Cisco sells the network. The network sees every packet that moves. Splunk reads every record that is written. Put them together and you have a view of an organisation’s actual behaviour that nobody else can assemble.

The timing is the part worth noticing. This was announced in the first full year of the AI boom, when companies were connecting systems they did not fully understand to models they understood less. The amount nobody could account for went up sharply, and the company that reads the record quietly became strategic.

Cisco paid cash in a year when cash was expensive and almost nobody else was buying anything.

What I would watch

Whether Cisco can sell a software subscription to a customer base that has bought hardware from it for thirty years. That is a different motion and the graveyard of hardware companies attempting it is well populated.

My read is that the asset was correctly identified and the integration is the open question, which is true of most large software acquisitions and is almost never what the announcement is argued about.

In a year when nobody could say what their own systems were doing, the company that keeps the record turned out to be infrastructure.
