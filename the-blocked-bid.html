TheBlocked Bid

A PRESIDENTIAL ORDER ENDS THE LARGEST
TECHNOLOGY TAKEOVER EVER ATTEMPTED

Francis Ruan

Broadcom went hostile for Qualcomm in November 2017 and raised its offer to around $117 billion, which would have been the largest technology acquisition in history by a distance. In March 2018 it was stopped by an executive order, on the recommendation of a committee most people in the industry could not have named a month earlier.

What was being bought

Qualcomm owns a great deal of the intellectual property underneath mobile communications. Not just chips: the patents on how a phone talks to a tower. Anybody building a modern handset pays Qualcomm something, which is a position roughly as good as ARM’s and considerably more litigated.

At the time, Qualcomm was deep in the work on 5G, the next generation of those standards. Standards are written in committees over years, and the companies with the most engineers in the room end up with the most patents in the result.

What Broadcom was going to do with it

Broadcom under Hock Tan runs a specific and very effective model. Buy something established, cut the research spending hard, keep the parts customers cannot leave, and return the cash. It works. It has made an enormous amount of money for shareholders over two decades.

Apply that to Qualcomm and you get a cost-efficient company with a smaller research budget, at the precise moment the 5G standards were being written.

Here is what was in the horse

The Committee on Foreign Investment in the United States looked at that sequence and reached a conclusion nobody in the deal had priced. If Qualcomm’s research spending fell, the companies filling the gap in those standards committees would be Chinese, principally Huawei. The acquisition would not transfer technology to a rival. It would create a vacancy.

Broadcom was then domiciled in Singapore, which gave the committee jurisdiction. On 12 March 2018 a presidential order prohibited the transaction. Not on antitrust grounds. On national security grounds.

This is the moment chips stopped being a product and became territory. Everything that followed, the export controls, the entity lists, the subsidies, the fabs under construction in Arizona and Dresden and Kumamoto, was already inside that decision.

My read

Dealmakers took a narrow lesson from this, which was to check the jurisdiction early. I think the wider lesson is more uncomfortable.

For thirty years the industry operated on the assumption that capital was the senior party and governments administered the paperwork. That assumption ended on a Monday in March 2018, and a great many of the deals further down this spine died for the same reason, in the same chair, with much better lawyers.

A hundred and seventeen billion dollars was stopped by an argument about who would be sitting in a standards committee in five years. Nobody in the room had modelled that line.
