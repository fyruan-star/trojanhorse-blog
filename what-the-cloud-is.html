WhatThe Cloud Is

IT IS A BUILDING, IT HAS AN ADDRESS
AND SOMEBODY PAYS ITS POWER BILL

Francis Ruan

The cloud is somebody else’s computer in a building you will never visit. That is not a simplification to be nice to you. That is literally what it is, and holding onto that one fact will carry you through most of the last fifteen years.

The thing that actually changed

Before roughly 2010, if you ran a company and needed computing, you bought computers. You put them in a room, hired people to look after them, and wrote a very large cheque up front for capacity you would mostly not use.

Then Amazon, which had built a lot of computers for its shop, noticed it could rent out the spare ones. You stopped buying the computers and started renting them by the hour.

In accounting terms, a large one-off purchase became a monthly bill. That sounds like a technicality and it rearranged the entire industry, because a monthly bill is something a company with no money can afford. Two people in a flat could suddenly have the same computing power as a bank. Almost every software company you can name exists because of this one change.

There are three of them, and that is the problem

Amazon got there first and is still the largest. Microsoft came second and had an unfair advantage, which is that it was already inside every corporate building on earth selling them Office. Google came third and has stayed third.

Once your company’s data lives in one of them, moving it is not a decision, it is a project. It takes years, it costs a fortune, and nothing visibly improves when you are done. So people do not move. The industry word for this is lock-in, and it is the most valuable thing any of these companies owns.

Which is why multicloud is a fighting word

Large companies worked out the lock-in problem and started deliberately keeping things in more than one cloud, so no single provider could put the price up and be obeyed. That is multicloud.

It also creates a genuinely valuable position: the company whose software works across all three. If you sell something that runs everywhere, you get paid regardless of who wins, and you are the only one in the room who does not care.

Hold that thought. IBM paid $34bn for it in 2018 and Google paid $32bn for it in 2025, and the two deals are the same move wearing different clothes.

The cloud is a rental business, and the most valuable seat in a rental business is not owning the building. It is being the thing every tenant needs regardless of which building they are in.

Words you now own

  • HyperscalerOne of the three enormous cloud providers. Amazon, Microsoft, Google.
  • CapexMoney spent buying a thing you keep. The old way.
  • OpexMoney spent running the business month to month. The rental way.
  • Lock-inHow expensive it would be to leave. Usually the real asset.
  • MulticloudDeliberately using more than one, so none of them owns you.
