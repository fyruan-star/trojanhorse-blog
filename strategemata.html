M&A Advisory

The Mandate

Almost everything written about investment banking concerns the hours. This is about the part that determines whether there are any hours at all, which is how a bank ends up being the one in the room.

The work is sold before it exists

A company deciding whether to sell itself does not run a procurement process for advisers. By the time anyone outside knows there is a transaction, the bank has usually been chosen.

This is why banks organise themselves into coverage groups, with a team responsible for the companies in one industry. The team reads the filings, tracks the executives, and stays in contact for years without a fee attached. A managing director typically owns that relationship and is the one who receives the call.

League tables exist partly for this reason. A ranking of who advised on the most deals is a crude measure of quality, and research on adviser choice has found that it functions as a substitute for reputation when a client has no personal history with a firm.

Then there is the other way, which is lending

The more interesting answer is that a great many acquirers hire the bank that already lends to them.

Four economists, writing for the Federal Reserve Bank of New York in 2002, looked at what happens when a bank acts as both lender and adviser. The paper is Allen, Jagtiani, Peristiani and Saunders, and the finding is blunt. Acquirers tend to choose their own banks, meaning those with prior lending relationships, as advisers in mergers. The reason they give is that the acquirer wants access to the loans that will pay for the deal.

A commercial bank that has lent to a company for years holds information about it that nobody else has. It has seen the covenants, the drawdowns and the quarters that did not go well. The paper describes this as a certification function, where the bank vouches for a value an outsider cannot verify.

Certification works, but only in one direction

The same paper tested whether the certification holds, and the answer depends on which side of the table the bank is sitting.

For targets, it holds. A target earns higher abnormal returns when its own bank certifies its value to the acquirer. That makes sense, because the target is the thing being priced and it is the more opaque of the two.

For acquirers, it does not. The authors find no certification role, and write that conflicts of interest appear to dominate. The mechanism is simple enough to state in one sentence. The acquirer’s adviser is negotiating the terms of the merger and the terms of the future loan at the same time.

The market can see this. The paper reports significantly negative abnormal returns for bank advisers when they advise their own loan customers in acquiring other firms. The study is now more than twenty years old and the figures in it are historical, but the structure it describes is the one large banks still run.

Which is the whole pitch of the firms that do not lend

An entire category of firm exists because of the paragraph above.

Evercore’s own annual report describes the idea it was founded on as a firm free of the conflicts that arise in large, multi-product, capital-intensive institutions. It makes no use of its own balance sheet. Centerview, founded in 2006, runs the same model with no underwriting or lending arm at all.

The pitch is one sentence long. We cannot lend you anything, so nothing in this advice is about a loan.

The counter-pitch is also one sentence. A bulge bracket can advise the deal, underwrite the financing and hedge the exposure under one engagement, which matters a great deal when a transaction needs twenty billion dollars of debt on a schedule. Both pitches are honest. They are selling different things and the client is choosing which risk it would rather carry.

What the business looks like right now

After the quietest stretch since the financial crisis, the advisory market has recovered hard. Goldman Sachs reported record advisory revenues in the third quarter of 2026 and has advised on roughly $1.5 trillion of announced transactions this year, which is the top of the table by a distance.

David Solomon, speaking on the firm’s January 2026 call, said the world is set up at the moment to be incredibly constructive in 2026 for M&A and capital markets. Goldman’s published outlook expects global volumes to pass the 2021 record and reach about $3.8 trillion.

I would read chief executive commentary on a pipeline with some care. A bank forecasting a strong year for dealmaking is forecasting demand for its own product. The league table figures are more checkable, and they vary by data provider, which is worth knowing before anyone quotes one at you.

Why this is a strategy question and not an etiquette one

Put the pieces together and the shape of the industry follows from them.

If acquirers hire their lenders, then the bank with the largest balance sheet has a structural lead on buy-side work, and the firms without one compete for sell-side mandates where independence is the product. That is not a cultural difference between institutions. It is an arithmetic one.

It also explains why banks pay so much for senior hires and why those moves are reported the way transfers are. A managing director arriving from a competitor brings the relationships that decide who gets called, and those take a decade to build and about a week to move.

The clearest case I have looked at is BofA Securities, whose public record across 2025 and 2026 is almost entirely buy-side in technology. I wrote that up separately.

A client picks the adviser before there is anything to advise on, and a great many acquirers pick the institution that is already lending to them.
