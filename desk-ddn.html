DDN takes $300m fromBlackstone

A STORAGE COMPANY THAT STOPPED SELLING CAPACITY
AND STARTED SELLING UTILIZATION

Francis Ruan

For twenty-six years two founders owned a storage company outright. Alex Bouzari and Paul Bloch started DDN in 1998, built it into a profitable business and took no outside money. In January 2025 Blackstone Tactical Opportunities put in $300 million at a $5 billion valuation. It was the first outside capital DDN had accepted.

A company that never needed anyone

A company that has never needed a bank is the hardest kind to advise. There is no failing business to repair and no deadline forcing a decision. The only question is the one founders dislike most, which is why now, and why this investor.

What the bank did

Several outlets reported that BofA Securities advised DDN. What follows describes how an assignment like this generally runs and is not a record of the room.

The work begins with a story the founders had never needed to tell. Twenty-six years of quiet, profitable growth has to be turned into a case an investor can underwrite in a few weeks. Then the bank decides whom to call. A strategic buyer would want control, and these founders did not want to sell. A growth fund might want a board seat and a decade-long hold. A group like Blackstone Tactical Opportunities can write a large cheque, accept a minority position and move quickly.

The part that decides the outcome is the price and what the founders keep in exchange for it. Here they kept the company. They sold a slice of a business they still run. Most of an adviser’s work in that position is translation. Founders talk about customers and product. Investors talk about growth, margin and what year five looks like. The bank holds both conversations and makes them meet at a number.

Why $5 billion

The announcement did not publish a revenue figure. Forbes has reported roughly $400 million of revenue in 2024, about $500 million in 2025 and a projection near $1 billion for 2026. Those are reported figures and not audited ones.

On those numbers $5 billion is about ten times this year’s revenue and about five times next year’s if the projection holds. That is a fair price for a business growing at that pace and an unusual one for this part of the market, where most companies selling into AI infrastructure spend more than they earn. DDN has been profitable for decades.

The rest of the price is proximity. DDN says its systems support more than 500,000 NVIDIA GPUs, including xAI’s Colossus cluster, and that is a company claim and not a measured market share. A company sitting next to that much spending gets valued as part of it. Blackstone already owns data centres through QTS, so a storage business inside the same flow of capital is a natural second position.

What it does, twice

In technical terms, DDN builds high-performance parallel storage. Its systems let thousands of compute nodes read and write the same dataset at once, using file systems such as Lustre. Its newer Infinia platform is a software-defined data layer aimed at AI workloads, where the job is keeping processors fed and organizing the data for training and inference.

In plain terms, picture a kitchen with a thousand chefs, each costing tens of thousands of dollars. If the groceries arrive through one narrow door, most of the chefs stand around. The kitchen is not the problem. The door is. DDN builds the door.

However

However, the interesting thing about DDN is what it stopped optimizing.

For forty years the storage industry asked one question, which was how to hold more bytes more cheaply. Disk gave way to flash and flash got denser. Every improvement was a faster horse.

Elon Musk’s habit of walking into an industry and scratching its premise is the right lens. Cars did not beat horses by being better horses, and planes did not beat cars by being better cars. They changed what the machine was for. AI infrastructure did that to storage. The new question is not how much it can hold. It is how many dollars of processor time are idle while it waits for data. Once that is the measure, a storage company is not selling capacity. It is selling utilization on the most expensive chips ever built.

There is a small irony in the customer list. The most famous practitioner of scratching the premise runs a supercomputer on a system built for the new one. That reframing is also why $300 million at $5 billion is not a storage multiple. It is priced as a piece of the GPU economy. The shorter note on this deal, which covers the economics, is in the Ten Years series.

Whether it holds

There are three reasons it might. DDN has decades of profit, so it can fund its own product cycle, and it now has fresh capital for Infinia. It has deep roots in high-performance computing, where a storage failure costs weeks of training and buyers do not switch vendors lightly. And data has gravity. Once petabytes live in a system, moving them is slow and risky.

There are three reasons it might not. Customer concentration, because a few very large AI buyers drive the growth. The GPU spending cycle, because if it slows storage orders slow with it. And the possibility that hyperscalers or NVIDIA’s own reference designs pull storage in-house or toward a short list of approved vendors.

Who it fights

From my own reading and not from the press coverage, the competitors are VAST Data, which is the most aggressive private rival, along with WEKA, Pure Storage, NetApp, Dell’s PowerScale line, IBM Storage Scale, HPE’s Cray storage line and Hammerspace. DDN’s edge is a long record inside the largest and most demanding clusters.

What the record does not show

The revenue and ownership figures above come from Forbes by way of secondary outlets, and I could not open the primary page. BofA’s exact role is reported but not detailed, so the account of the work is mine.

What a storage company is worth depends on how much idle processor time it removes, and much less on how many bytes it holds.
