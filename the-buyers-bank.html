TheBuyer’s Bank

ON 57 COMPANIES, SIX TECHNOLOGY NAMES
AND WHAT A COVERAGE DESK ACTUALLY SELLS

Francis Ruan

The question cannot be answered the way it was asked, and that is the most useful thing about it. No SEC filing names an office. A merger proxy tells you that BofA Securities delivered the opinion and roughly what it was paid; it does not tell you whether the work happened in Palo Alto, New York or London. There is no public ledger of what a coverage desk did last year. So I went at the record that does exist, and the shape of that record turned out to be the answer. My read is that BofA in technology is the buyer’s bank, and that this is a structural position rather than a slow year.

What is actually in the record

I pulled every SEC filing from January 2025 through September 2026 that names BofA Securities as financial adviser or carries its fairness opinion. Fifty-seven distinct companies. Look at the composition before I say anything about it.

Twelve real estate names and REITs. Nine transport and business services. Eight healthcare. Four utilities. Eighteen industrial, consumer, energy and financial. Six at technology SIC codes.

Six out of fifty-seven. That is the franchise the public record describes, and what it describes is a real assets and utilities house with an exceptionally good research desk attached. Paramount Group, Rayonier, Americold, PotlatchDeltic, LXP, Whitestone, Peakstone, Sila, Apollo Commercial. American Water and Essential Utilities. NextEra. Dominion. And Union Pacific with Norfolk Southern, which at $84.4bn is the largest item on the list by a distance, and which is a railroad.

The six technology names are not the six you would guess

TaskUs and TELUS Digital are both business process outsourcing. On TaskUs, BofA advised the buyer group, Blackstone alongside the two co-founders, on a $16.50 per share take-private worth roughly $1.62bn announced in May 2025. Evercore sat with the special committee. On TELUS Digital, BofA was engaged by the special committee in July 2025 for a $4.50 per share squeeze-out by the parent, aggregate consideration around $539m.

Sabre is a defense mandate. BofA advised on the limited-duration rights plan the board adopted in March 2026 after Constellation Software built a position, and in September it was dealer manager on the secured-debt tenders. TruBridge is a healthcare IT strategic review.

LiveRamp is the San Francisco data company Publicis agreed to buy in May 2026 at $38.50 a share, $2.167bn of enterprise value. BofA advised Publicis. The French advertising holding company, not the California software company. Zonda is the same shape: $800m, closed August 2026, BofA on the CoStar side, which is to say the buyer, out of Arlington.

Read the six together and something shows up that I do not think is accidental. Not one of them is a founder deciding to sell.

And the best mandate is not in the filings at all

Here is where my method breaks, and I would rather break it myself than have you do it for me. Wiz was private, so Google’s $32bn acquisition of it produced no proxy, no fairness opinion and no EDGAR hit. It is the largest acquisition of a private technology company ever completed. It was announced in March 2025. BofA Securities was exclusive financial adviser on it.

To Google. Goldman had Wiz.

That one mandate is worth more to a franchise than most of the fifty-seven combined and I am not going to pretend otherwise. But look at which chair it was. The largest technology transaction of the period, and BofA sat with the acquirer, while the company being sold, the thing the founders had built and were choosing to part with, hired someone else.

Which makes the pattern the product, not the accident

So here is the value proposition as the record describes it rather than as a pitchbook would. A coverage desk that is genuinely excellent at representing people deploying capital. Sponsors running take-privates. Strategics buying data assets. Boards defending against an accumulator. A universal bank with a balance sheet behind the advice, which is a real product and one that Qatalyst and Centerview cannot offer at any price.

What it does not appear to sell is the seat next to a founder in the week they decide.

I want to be precise about why that gap matters, because the usual version of this argument is just league-table snobbery. A buy-side mandate is a corporate relationship. It renews on the acquirer’s schedule, it survives banker turnover, and it is in the end a procurement decision made by a corporate development team who will run the same process next time. A sell-side mandate is a personal one. It attaches to a human being at the most consequential moment of their working life, and that person then founds something else, joins three boards, writes angel checks and eventually partner checks.

The buy-side relationship ends at closing. The sell-side one follows somebody around Silicon Valley for thirty years.

What interests me here is that technology is close to the only market where that asymmetry is this extreme. Sell a railroad and the seller retires. Sell a company at thirty-two and you are a repeat player for four decades, and every one of those decades has a banker attached to it.

The strongest argument against me

The obvious objection is that my instrument is bad, and it partly is. EDGAR only sees transactions with a US public filer. Silicon Valley’s deal count is overwhelmingly private targets, which generate nothing at all. Fairness opinions are mostly target-side, so buy-side work is structurally undercounted. Wiz is both failures in one example.

Two things survive it. The undercount runs toward missing sell-side public deals, and BofA’s public sell-side work is not missing, it is abundant. It is just abundant in REITs. Twelve real estate names and no venture-backed software company is a composition finding rather than a coverage gap in my data. And the large technology sales that did surface went elsewhere with some consistency. Palo Alto Networks did the largest security deal of the period at about $25bn and used JPMorgan, while CyberArk used Qatalyst. Confluent sold to IBM at $11.9bn with Morgan Stanley on the target.

My concern is not that BofA had a bad year. It was top five in the Americas and it advised the target on an $84.4bn railroad merger, which is a serious piece of work by any measure. My concern is narrower and I think more serious. A franchise can be top five by value and still not be in the room where the decision gets made.

Which is exactly what March was about

In March 2026 Reuters reported four senior technology hires, sourced to internal memos. Gary Kirkham from Centerview as executive vice chair, a man who had already spent more than twenty years at BofA as global co-head of TMT. Jason Rowe from Goldman Sachs as global co-head of technology investment banking, one of three software co-heads there across two decades. Mahir Zaimoglu and Patrik Czornik from JPMorgan, both in London. It followed Kevin Brunner leaving for JPMorgan as global chairman of investment banking and M&A, and Ric Spencer leaving for Citigroup in October.

I do not think you hire a Goldman software co-head for his execution. Execution is the thing BofA has demonstrably got, and the Wiz mandate proves it at the highest possible difficulty. You hire him for his phone, and specifically for twenty years of founders in it who will take his call in the week they are deciding.

Which is the bank agreeing with the diagnosis. The hires are the admission.

What I would watch

  • Whether a venture-backed sell-side mandate appears in the next four quarters, because that is the only evidence that will show the hires worked.
  • Whether the balance sheet keeps winning sponsor take-privates, which is the part of the franchise that already functions and gets almost no attention.
  • Whether Rowe’s first mandates are Google-shaped or founder-shaped.
  • Whether the Palo Alto seat itself grows, given that two of the four hires sit in London and the third sits in New York.

That last one is the tell I would trust most. A bank that intends to win founder mandates in Northern California eventually has to put senior people in Northern California, and so far the announced geography says London and New York.

A buy-side mandate closes with the deal. A sell-side one follows a person around for thirty years, and that is the thing a coverage desk is really being asked to sell.

Sources and method

  1. The fifty-seven companies come from EDGAR full-text search, 1 January 2025 to 28 September 2026, on the phrases “Opinion of BofA Securities” and the several “is acting as / is serving as / acted as financial advisor” constructions, deduplicated by filer. Sector counts use each filer’s SIC code. This is a floor, not a census: it can only see filings that use those phrases.
  2. Google and Wiz, $32bn, announced 18 March 2025. BofA Securities as exclusive financial adviser to Google and Goldman Sachs to Wiz, per transaction records and contemporaneous trade coverage. Fenwick, lead counsel to Wiz, describes it as the largest acquisition of a private technology company ever.
  3. TaskUs, announced 9 May 2025: $16.50 per share, roughly $1.62bn, BofA Securities to the buyer group (Blackstone with co-founders Bryce Maddock and Jaspar Weir), Evercore to the special committee. Company announcement and related filings.
  4. TELUS Digital: the special committee engaged BofA Securities as independent financial adviser on 6 July 2025, with BMO as independent valuator. US$4.50 per share, aggregate consideration around US$539m. Approved by shareholders 27 October 2025.
  5. Publicis Groupe and LiveRamp, announced 17 May 2026: $38.50 per share, $2.167bn enterprise value, BofA Securities to Publicis. CoStar Group and Zonda, $800m, completed 21 August 2026, BofA Securities to CoStar.
  6. Sabre: BofA Securities named as financial adviser on the limited-duration shareholder rights plan adopted March 2026, and as dealer manager on the September 2026 secured-debt tender offers.
  7. The four hires and the two departures: Reuters, 10 March 2026, sourced to two internal memos. Titles and locations as reported there.
  8. League-table context from Mergermarket / ION Analytics 1H26. Union Pacific and Norfolk Southern at $84.4bn with BofA on the target, from the transaction record and the merger filings.

Office-level attribution is not public anywhere. No filing, press release or league table says which desk staffed which deal, so nothing here should be read as a claim about who specifically worked on what. The argument is about the composition of a franchise, which is observable, and not about the performance of any individual or team, which is not.
