Trojan Horse of the Week1 November 2026

Uber Invites Its Rival to Dinner

A good quarter and a worse mood

Picture the morning of Wednesday 5 August 2026. Uber had just reported a quarter most companies would frame and hang in the lobby (the earnings release). Gross bookings were $58.0 billion, up 24%. Net income was $2.4 billion, including a $1.6 billion pre-tax gain from revaluing its investments. Free cash flow over the trailing twelve months passed $10 billion for the first time in the company’s history. By the close the shares were down 5.3%, and CNBC reported they had been down 12% for the year while the Nasdaq was up 14%. I love a company that does its worrying from a position of strength. It resembles a person with a corner office, a good salary and a sudden interest in sailing, and the use of a midlife crisis is that it reveals what the person is afraid of.

What the annual report admits

Uber is afraid of cars that drive themselves, and it says so in writing. In its annual report it names Alphabet’s Waymo, Amazon’s Zoox and Tesla among the companies that either compete with it or may compete with it in the future. The risk factors add that Waymo has already put a commercial fleet on its own platform, that rivals could deploy autonomous vehicles on platforms other than Uber’s, and that Uber’s own partners could remove their vehicles from its network.

Waymo is also one of those partners. In Austin and Atlanta its cars can be hailed only through Uber’s app, according to Waymo’s own page, and in most of its other cities Waymo runs its own app. Imagine a restaurant that rents its dining room to a chef who is opening a place across the street, and then add that the chef is very good.

The threat is real and small at once. Waymo reported 500,000 paid rides a week across ten U.S. cities in March and has talked about reaching a million by the end of the year (Bloomberg). Uber does roughly 300 million trips a week, which puts Waymo’s rides at about one in 600 of Uber’s volume by my arithmetic, and Uber says autonomous trips of every kind are under 0.5% of its total. Its chief executive says trips grew faster in markets where robotaxis are widely available through Uber (CNBC). That is Uber’s argument, that the cars are supply and supply fills demand. The annual report worries about the reverse, a supply that learns to find the demand without Uber.

Move one: open every door

On 23 February 2026 Uber launched Uber Autonomous Solutions, a menu of services for anyone building a robotaxi. It covers data and mapping, the software a rider sees inside the car, and what Uber calls fleet operations: a mission-control system that shows operators every vehicle, remote assistance, field teams that handle lost items, towing and cleaning, and insurance. The partners include Nuro and Lucid, Volkswagen, Avride, Wayve, WeRide and NVIDIA. Its chief executive has said Uber expects to commit more than $10 billion over the coming years, and in the second-quarter release its chief executive, Dara Khosrowshahi, said Uber is building “the world’s largest platform for autonomous vehicles.”

There is a logic to it. A company that builds a self-driving car is good at software and short of exactly what Uber already has, which is riders, field crews who will clean a car at midnight, and an insurer. Uber is offering a new robotaxi maker customers and the unglamorous work in one contract. That is the horse. The partners arrive as gifts, new supply with no driver attached, and the exposure comes through the same gate, since the 10-K says those vehicles can leave. Uber and Waymo have said they will end their exclusive arrangement in Atlanta and Austin by early 2028, which CNBC described as Waymo pulling away.

Move two: buy dinner

On 16 July 2026 Uber offered €41.50 a share in cash for Delivery Hero, the Berlin-based food delivery group. That values the whole company at $14.8 billion, or $13.7 billion once the stake Uber had already bought is netted out. It was no stranger’s bid. Uber already held about 25% of the voting shares and about 12% more through derivatives, and its 10-Q shows $1.6 billion of total return swaps on Delivery Hero stock in the second quarter. Prosus has agreed to tender its remaining stake of about 17%, which would bring Uber’s economic interest to about 53%.

The offer needs 50% plus one share and merger-control and financial regulatory clearances, and Uber expects to close in the second half of 2027. It will pay from cash and new debt, with a committed bridge facility of about €14 billion, and it says it expects to keep an investment grade rating and gross leverage below 2x. Uber’s pitch is breadth. The combined company would operate in 99 markets with pro forma 2025 gross bookings of $236 billion, and the number of markets where it offers both rides and delivery would rise from 34 to 58. Uber says customers who use both services generate about three times the gross bookings and profit of customers who use one.

Dinner is already close to the size of the ride. In the second quarter delivery gross bookings were $27.5 billion against $29.0 billion for mobility, and delivery grew faster, 26% to 22%. A robotaxi’s job description is moving a person. Waymo’s one active delivery operation, as of its October 2025 announcement, was a Phoenix pilot with DoorDash, Uber Eats’ rival, carrying orders from DoorDash’s own stores. Citizens’ Andrew Boone, as Parameter reports it, holds that delivery has a stronger competitive position than mobility.

What Uber has to trade with

Uber has three things a robotaxi company lacks, and each shows in the filings. The first is the name. In the second quarter 208 million people used the platform in a month, up 16%, and they took 3.9 billion trips. The second is cash. Free cash flow was $2.8 billion in the quarter, the company spent $3.5 billion on buybacks in the first six months, and it has lined up a bridge for the Delivery Hero offer without changing its buyback framework.

The third is people. Uber has drivers and couriers, and a business built around the unglamorous work of cars in the physical world. Its data-services unit, Uber AI Solutions, offers drivers small digital tasks when they are offline, such as recording audio samples or uploading menus, and Uber’s product chief said it was a way to keep drivers earning and not a response to being displaced by autonomous vehicles (Bloomberg, via the Inquirer). The field teams that clean and tow robotaxis apply the same idea to a different customer.

The case against

The honest worry comes in two parts. The first is the borrowing. A $14.8 billion offer funded partly by new debt, with a closing a year away, is a commitment made while the stock is down. Uber is also promising no workforce changes at the Berlin headquarters until at least 2029 and a €2 billion investment in Germany over five years, which narrows where it can find savings.

The second is that dinner does not hedge the ride. Rides remain the larger business, the 10-K says its autonomous partners could leave or compete, and delivery has its own robotaxi pilot under way, run by a competitor. A commitment of more than $10 billion to partners that may not stay is a bet on many horses, and the document that describes the bet also describes the exit.

What to watch

Four things can be checked in public. The first is what happens to Waymo on Uber’s app once the exclusive arrangement in Austin and Atlanta ends by early 2028. The second is how the Delivery Hero offer moves through acceptance and clearances during the second half of 2027. The third is whether autonomous trips climb past the 0.5% of Uber’s volume that Uber reports today. The fourth is whether delivery keeps growing faster than rides, 26% against 22% in the latest quarter. A company can be worried and well run at the same moment, and these four numbers show which of the two is gaining.

Company figures come from Uber’s second-quarter 2026 earnings release, its 2025 annual report, its second-quarter 10-Q and its Delivery Hero announcement and Autonomous Solutions release. Waymo’s ride count is from TechCrunch, its year-end goal is from Bloomberg’s headline, and its Uber arrangement is from Waymo’s own page. The analyst view is as reported by Parameter and was not checked against Citizens’ own note. The share of Uber’s volume that Waymo represents is my arithmetic from a March figure and an August one, so it is a scale and not a measurement. The notes this story grew from called Uber distressed. Its filings show record cash flow, so the story treats the worry as strategic.
