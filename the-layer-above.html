IBM andRed Hat

A SUBSCRIPTION BUSINESS BUILT ON SOFTWARE
THAT ANYONE CAN DOWNLOAD FOR NOTHING

Francis Ruan

IBM agreed to acquire Red Hat in October 2018 at $190 a share in cash, approximately $34 billion including assumed debt and a premium above 60 percent. It completed in July 2019. Red Hat's principal product is free software.

What customers are paying for

Red Hat Enterprise Linux is a distribution of Linux, which is open source. The code is available at no charge and can be downloaded, modified and redistributed by anyone.

What Red Hat sold was a supported version. Tested across a defined set of hardware, patched on a published schedule for a long support window, and certified to work with the commercial applications a bank or hospital runs. The subscription buys accountability and a maintenance commitment rather than access to the software.

The strength of that position comes from work the customer has already done. An institution that has certified several hundred applications against one distribution would have to repeat that testing to move. The cost is counted in engineering years and in the risk of an outage, not in the licence fee of an alternative.

Why IBM was the buyer

IBM had lost ground in cloud computing to Amazon and Microsoft over the preceding years and had no realistic path to catching them by building. Buying a fourth-place cloud provider would have been expensive and would not have addressed the problem.

Red Hat offered something different. Its software is used by enterprises across more than one cloud provider and in their own facilities, so its revenue does not depend on which provider wins.

The open question

IBM's share price has not clearly reflected the purchase in the years since, which is the objection usually raised. A more specific question is more useful.

The value of software whose selling point is that it works equally well everywhere depends on customers believing it will continue to. IBM also sells a cloud service. Whether an owner in that position can maintain the neutrality the asset relies on is a question about organisational behaviour, and no single year of results settles it.

Where a customer has spent years certifying against one supplier, the supplier's pricing power comes from that work rather than from the product being better.
