Fetching

THE MOST EXPENSIVE PROCESSOR EVER BUILT
SPENDS A GREAT DEAL OF ITS LIFE WAITING

Francis Ruan

Blackstone put $300 million into DataDirect Networks in January 2025 at a $5 billion valuation. DDN is not a startup. It was founded in 1998 and has spent a quarter of a century selling storage into supercomputing centres, in a market so unfashionable that almost nobody outside it could name a competitor.

The thing nobody says about a GPU

A GPU does not think slowly. It waits.

Picture the most expensive processor ever manufactured at scale, sitting idle, because the data it needs to look at next has not arrived from storage yet. That is the constraint. Not intelligence. Not even electricity, though that is coming. Fetching.

The bottleneck in artificial intelligence turns out to be the same one a library has, which is that it does not matter how many books you own if it takes forty minutes to find one.

So the money went to the corridor

When Blackstone writes a cheque for the company that shortens the walk to the shelf, it is not investing in artificial intelligence. It is investing in the corridor.

Corridors are historically where the durable money is. Nobody remembers who wrote the songs. Everybody remembers who owned the radio station.

Here is what was in the horse

DDN was founded in 1998. So was Google.

One of them became a verb. The other spent twenty-seven years quietly selling fast storage to national laboratories and weather modellers and genomics institutes. No keynote. No documentary.

Then the machines got hungry, the unfashionable thing turned out to be the constraint, and a twenty-seven-year-old company woke up worth five billion dollars.

I find this the best argument available against the idea that you have to be early. You have to be correct, and then you have to still be standing there when the world finally needs the thing you have been quietly good at the whole time. Patience is a strategy. It has terrible marketing.

And the rest of the pattern

Once you see it you cannot unsee it. Cheap object storage raising at scale. Data protection being bought. Seagate, which makes the spinning platters everybody declared obsolete a decade ago, raising money on terms usually offered to growth companies.

Hard drives were supposed to be dead. Flash was faster, had no moving parts, and that was meant to be the end of it. Then machines that read everything needed somewhere enormous and cheap to keep what they might want later, and slow but vast became a category again.

Rust is back. Nobody put that on a slide in 2015.

The capital markets have just told us, in the most expensive language available to them, what they think intelligence is made of. Not cleverness. The speed of the walk to the shelf.
