WhatThe Cloud Is

A BUILDING WITH AN ADDRESS, OWNED BY SOMEBODY ELSE,
RENTED BY THE HOUR

Francis Ruan

The cloud is somebody else's computer, in a building you will not visit, rented by the hour. That is not a simplification. Holding on to it will carry you through a good deal of what follows.

What changed around 2010

Before then, a company that needed computing bought computers. It put them in a room, employed people to maintain them, and paid up front for capacity it would mostly not use.

Amazon, which had built a great deal of computing capacity for its retail business, began renting the spare capacity to others. Microsoft and Google followed.

In accounting terms a large one-off purchase became a monthly expense. That sounds like a technicality and it changed who could start a software company. Two people now had access to the same computing capacity as a bank, and paid only for what they used. A great many software companies founded after 2010 exist in a form that would not have been affordable before it.

Why there are three

Amazon arrived first and remains the largest. Microsoft was second and had an advantage, in that it was already selling software to most large organisations. Google has been third for most of the period covered here.

Once a company's systems and data are established with one provider, moving them is a project rather than a decision. It takes a long time, it costs a great deal, and little visibly improves when it is finished. So customers tend to stay. The term for this is lock-in, and it is usually the most valuable thing a provider has.

Multicloud, and why customers want it

Larger customers recognised the position they were in and began keeping systems with more than one provider deliberately, so that no single supplier could raise prices without a credible response. That practice is called multicloud.

It also creates a position worth having. A supplier whose software works across all three providers earns revenue regardless of which one a customer chooses, and has no stake in the outcome.

IBM paid about $34 billion for a company in that position in 2018, and Google agreed to pay about $32 billion for another in 2025. The resemblance between those two purchases is worth noticing when you reach them.

Renting computing changed who could afford to start a software company. It also created a durable position for suppliers whose products work across all three major providers.

Words you now own

  • HyperscalerOne of the very large cloud providers. Amazon, Microsoft, Google.
  • CapexMoney spent acquiring an asset the company keeps.
  • OpexMoney spent running the business period to period.
  • Lock-inWhat it would cost a customer to leave. Often the real asset.
  • MulticloudUsing more than one provider deliberately, to preserve leverage.
