Broadcom andQualcomm

A TAKEOVER PROHIBITED ON NATIONAL SECURITY
GROUNDS RATHER THAN COMPETITION GROUNDS

Francis Ruan

Broadcom pursued Qualcomm from November 2017 without the agreement of its board, raising its offer to around $117 billion. In March 2018 a presidential order prohibited the transaction, following a review by the Committee on Foreign Investment in the United States.

What Qualcomm owns

Qualcomm sells mobile processors, and it also holds a large body of patents covering how a handset communicates with a network. Manufacturers pay licence fees on those patents, a business that has been both consistently profitable and frequently litigated.

In 2017 and 2018 Qualcomm was among the most active participants in the committee work producing the 5G standards. Standards are negotiated over years, and companies contributing more engineers tend to hold more of the patents that end up essential to the result.

Why the review reached the conclusion it did

Broadcom's record with companies it acquires is public. It reduces research spending, concentrates on the most profitable product lines, and returns cash to shareholders. Applied to Qualcomm, the committee's view was that this would reduce American participation in the standards process and that other participants, principally Huawei, would fill the gap.

Broadcom was domiciled in Singapore at the time, which gave the committee jurisdiction. Broadcom disputed the analysis and offered commitments on research spending. The order was issued regardless.

What changed as a result

Before this, large technology acquisitions were reviewed principally for their effect on competition. Afterward, the relevant question for semiconductor transactions included who would hold a position in a future standard, and that question is answered by governments rather than by markets.

Subsequent export controls, entity listings and manufacturing subsidies followed similar reasoning. I would be careful about treating the 2018 order as their cause. It is better described as the first occasion on which that reasoning was applied at this scale, in public.

The practical consequence for a buyer

Approval risk in deals of this size is no longer confined to an antitrust analysis a bidder can model. It now includes a national interest assessment that may turn on a forecast of industrial capability several years out. That is difficult to price, and it is one reason large semiconductor agreements carry substantial termination fees and long outside dates.

Approval risk in semiconductors stopped being a question about market share and became a question about national capability, which a bidder cannot model in the same way.

Sources

  1. Broadcom Limited's unsolicited proposals for Qualcomm Incorporated from November 2017, raised to approximately $121bn including assumed debt in February 2018 and subsequently reduced to around $117bn.
  2. Presidential Order of 12 March 2018 prohibiting the proposed takeover, on the recommendation of the Committee on Foreign Investment in the United States.
  3. The reasoning concerning 5G standards participation is the committee's, as set out in its correspondence and the order. Broadcom disputed it publicly at the time.
