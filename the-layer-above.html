TheLayer Above

IBM PAYS $34BN FOR A COMPANY
THAT GIVES ITS SOFTWARE AWAY

Francis Ruan

IBM agreed to buy Red Hat in October 2018 for $190 a share in cash, about $34 billion, a premium north of 60 percent and the largest software acquisition anybody had made. Red Hat’s core product is free. You can download it this afternoon.

How you sell something free

Red Hat’s business was built on Linux, which is open source. Anybody can use it, change it, and redistribute it, without paying.

What Red Hat sold was not the software. It sold a version somebody would stand behind: tested, patched, supported, and certified to work with the other things a bank or a hospital runs. Large institutions do not buy software. They buy somebody to be responsible when it breaks at two in the morning.

Once a bank has standardised on your distribution, changing it means recertifying every application it runs. That is a multi-year project with no visible benefit. So nobody does it.

Why IBM, specifically, and why then

IBM had been losing for a decade. It had missed the cloud, badly, and watched Amazon and Microsoft take a market it had every structural advantage in. Buying its way to a fourth-place cloud would have been expensive and pointless.

So it did not. It bought the position above the clouds instead.

Here is what was in the horse

Red Hat’s actual bet, which IBM paid $34 billion to own, was that no serious enterprise would put everything in one cloud. They would keep some workloads in Amazon, some in Microsoft, some in their own buildings, for reasons of price, regulation and plain negotiating leverage.

If that is true, there is enormous value in the layer that makes all of those look the same to the software running on top. You get paid regardless of who wins the cloud war, and you are the only party in the room with no preference about the outcome.

That bet was correct. It is also, and I do not think this gets said enough, exactly the move Google made seven years later when it paid $32 billion for Wiz, security instead of operating systems, the same structural position, within two billion dollars of the same price.

Where I would push back on myself

The obvious objection is that IBM paid a 60 percent premium and its share price has not obviously rewarded it, which is fair and is the number people point at.

My concern is different. The position was right. What I am less sure about is whether a company with IBM’s reflexes could hold it, because the value of being the neutral layer depends entirely on remaining neutral, and neutrality is not a thing large organisations are naturally good at.

When you cannot win the market, the next best thing is to own the part everyone has to cross regardless of who does.

Sources

  1. IBM and Red Hat joint announcement, 28 October 2018. $190.00 per share in cash, approximately $34bn including assumed debt. Closed July 2019.
  2. Red Hat's subscription and support model, and its enterprise certification position, from the company's own filings prior to acquisition.
  3. The comparison to Google and Wiz is my argument, not a claim about either company's reasoning.
