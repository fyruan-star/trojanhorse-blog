Sell-side M&A

How a Company Gets Sold

A sale process is a sequence built to control what each buyer knows and when, and to make buyers compete. Before any of it starts, somebody has to be chosen to run it, and that choice is made long before anything is for sale.

A software company agrees to buy a competitor for $5 billion. The announcement explains the price and the strategic rationale and, near the bottom, names the investment banks that advised each side. Each of those banks had to win the assignment, which is called the mandate, by persuading a company to trust it with a decision that could change its future.

An owner could sell a business without help, and very few do. Selling requires knowing what the business is worth, who would want it and what separates it from the other companies a buyer is looking at that quarter. Those are full-time questions, and the owner already has a full-time job. So a bank is hired to run the process.

My argument is that the standard sequence below is best understood as a set of solutions. The bake-off solves the problem of choosing an adviser whose quality cannot be tested. Preparation and the documents solve the problem of making the company legible to strangers. Marketing, meetings and bids solve the problem of finding the highest price without losing control of the information. Diligence and completion solve the problem of keeping a price once it has been agreed. The sequence is the standard version, and it usually runs from three months to several years. Not every sale follows it, and the last section uses a sale that did not.

This page runs in three parts. How the bank is chosen, which is settled before anything is for sale. What the bank then does, in order, with roughly how long each stage takes. And what both of those mean if you are nineteen and trying to decide which of these firms to join.

Part One. How the bank gets chosen

Two views of what an adviser is for

Independent advisory firms present objectivity as their main reason to be chosen. Evercore says in its annual report that it “was founded on the belief that there is an opportunity within the investment banking industry for a firm free of the potential conflicts of interest created within large, multi-product, capital intensive financial institutions” (Evercore 10-K). Centerview describes its advice as independent and “unencumbered by institutional constraints or conflicts of interest” (Centerview).

A study by four economists at the Federal Reserve Bank of New York points the other way. Linda Allen, Julapa Jagtiani, Stavros Peristiani and Anthony Saunders found in 2002 that “acquirers tend to choose their own banks” as merger advisers, meaning banks that already lent to them (New York Fed, Staff Report 143). The sample is historical, but it shows that a bank's lending relationship was commercially valuable to the client when it chose an adviser.

Both observations are accurate, and they describe different clients with different problems.

Why relationships decide

On a $5 billion purchase, a one percent difference in price is $50 million. A useful adviser might help the buyer avoid paying that extra amount, might recognize that the business deserves a higher price, or might tell the buyer to stop negotiating altogether. The client is paying for judgment whose quality becomes clear only after the decisions have been made. A polished presentation demonstrates preparation and shows little about how someone will behave when a negotiation turns difficult.

Relationships address that problem by giving the client evidence before the transaction. Banks organize teams around industries so their people stay close to potential clients. A banker covering software can study competitors, follow management changes and bring executives ideas long before there is a deal to discuss.

Consider two hypothetical bankers approaching the same chief executive. One arrives when a sale becomes likely. The other has spent years offering useful observations about the business, including reasons to reject earlier acquisition ideas. The second has given the executive more evidence on which to judge the advice. A formal pitch can still matter, but the relationship shapes how the client weighs what it hears.

Why financing changes the choice

Now suppose the buyer needs to borrow $2 billion to complete the acquisition. The company needs advice about the purchase and confidence that it can fund it. A large bank can offer both through different teams. Bank of America describes this structure on its own site: its Global Banking and Global Markets divisions operate under the Bank of America and BofA Securities names, with advisory work done by BofA Securities and lending by banking affiliates (business.bofa.com).

For the buyer, that combination reduces coordination problems, because a recommendation is more useful when the company already understands how it could pay for it. The New York Fed authors linked the choice of a lending bank to access to acquisition financing, which is the same advantage described from the client's side. My interpretation is that financing capability gives a bank another reason to be chosen, and that the size of the advantage depends on what the transaction requires.

A company's own board can state this logic in a filing. When Splunk chose advisers for its 2023 sale to Cisco, its proxy statement said it retained Morgan Stanley partly because the bank had “served as a lead underwriter on various Splunk capital markets transactions, including its initial public offering” (Splunk proxy statement). The board treated the bank's earlier work arranging money as a reason to trust its advice.

The conflict and the client's response

The same advantage creates a tension. If the bank earns fees from advising the software buyer and from arranging its debt, completing the acquisition produces revenue from both. The client wants the adviser to recommend whatever creates the most value, including walking away. The New York Fed authors identified conflicts associated with advising and lending to the same acquirer, including the bank's existing and prospective credit exposure.

Independence does not remove every incentive. Evercore states that it is “not paid until the successful consummation of the transactions,” and advisory fees that depend on completion give any adviser a reason to favor a deal that closes. The question a client can ask of every adviser is how it gets paid and how that could influence its recommendation.

A client can combine advantages. Our software buyer could hire an independent firm for acquisition advice and use a large bank for financing, or appoint several advisers with different strengths. A lender has the advantage when financing is difficult. An industry specialist has it when understanding the asset requires unusual expertise. A trusted adviser has it when a board faces a decision it has never encountered. A division of the market into large banks that advise buyers and independent firms that advise sellers misses this, because buyers need independent judgment, sellers may value a large bank's industry knowledge and relationships, and a particular banker's experience can outweigh the institution's model.

Reputation as a constraint

An adviser may gain from completing a transaction today. It also wants the client's next assignment and recommendations to other executives, and those future opportunities give it a reason to protect the quality of its advice. A recommendation to abandon a deal can therefore have commercial value, because the adviser gives up a fee and gives the client evidence that its judgment can be trusted.

That outcome depends on how much the adviser values future business, so reputation encourages good advice without guaranteeing it, and clients still need to evaluate each recommendation. It also helps explain why senior bankers matter so much to their firms. Evercore lists the departure of senior professionals as a risk that “could jeopardize our relationships with clients and result in the loss of client engagements and revenues.”

The strongest objection

The New York Fed study covers a historical sample, and the market has changed since. Boards now run formal competitions among advisers more often and hire several at once, which would reduce the weight of any one relationship. If that is right, lending relationships and coverage should predict mandates less than they once did.

That is testable. The adviser lists in merger filings show whether the bank that lent to an acquirer in the preceding years is still the one named on its acquisitions, and whether independent firms appear more often alongside the lenders than they did. A pattern of acquirers hiring their own lenders would support the argument here, and a pattern of unconnected advisers would weaken it.

Part Two. The eight stages

I Choosing the bank One to two weeks II Preparation Two to four months III The documents Runs alongside preparation IV Marketing Four to eight weeks V Management meetings Two to four weeks VI First bids One week, then a decision VII Final bids and negotiation Four to six weeks VIII Diligence and completion Two to four months

IOne to two weeks

Choosing the bank

Several banks are invited to present on why they should sell the company, an exercise the industry calls a bake-off. The problem it addresses is that the owner is paying for judgment whose quality cannot be checked in advance, so the pitch is a way to look for evidence of care and of understanding the business.

The outcome is often decided before the meeting. Senior bankers spend years in contact with owners who have nothing to sell, bringing them observations about competitors and about what similar businesses have fetched, so by the time a sale is real the owner often has a view already, for the reasons set out in Part One.

That does not make the meeting ceremonial. A managing director is paid on the mandates brought in, so the pitch decides a large share of several people's income for the year, and the presentation is built by analysts and associates in a week or two. Small errors matter for a reason of inference and not of vanity. An owner reading a hypothetical deck with misaligned logos may conclude the firm would be equally careless with the sale. Banks lose these competitions, and two weeks of work ends with no fee at all.

IITwo to four months

Preparation

Once the bank has won, nothing is for sale yet. The problem now is that a buyer will not trust a profit figure the seller prepared itself. An independent accounting firm is therefore brought in to examine the company's earnings, in what is called a quality of earnings review, which checks that the profit is real, repeatable and not the result of one unusual year. It plays the role of a structural survey commissioned before listing a house, so that the buyer's own surveyor finds nothing the seller has not already seen.

While that runs, the bank assembles answers to the questions every buyer asks, which run to several hundred. They cover financial statements and projections, customer contracts, legal standing, litigation and how concentrated the customer base is. Some answers are so routine that their absence becomes the finding, because a buyer who asks for revenue by customer and does not receive it concludes either that the company does not know or that it would prefer not to say.

A hypothetical example shows the kind of work involved. A buyer wants to know customer concentration, repeat purchase rates and geographic spread. The company has never produced that analysis. What it can produce is a file of every transaction it has recorded, and someone spends two days turning it into three charts. The financial model is the visible deliverable, and much of the effort goes into converting the company's records into something a stranger can read.

IIIRuns alongside preparation

The documents

Three documents carry the sale, and they are written in a deliberate order of disclosure so that the seller reveals more only as a buyer shows more interest. The teaser is one or two pages and usually does not name the company. It describes the business well enough for a buyer to know whether it is interested, and not well enough to identify it. The confidential information memorandum, called the CIM, runs fifty pages or more and is the full argument: what the company does, why it wins, what the numbers have been doing and where growth is supposed to come from. The management presentation is the deck the chief executive uses to talk through the business in a meeting.

All three are marketing documents and also the company's formal argument about itself. Writing them well requires understanding both what the business does and what a particular kind of buyer is listening for.

IVFour to eight weeks

Marketing

The teaser goes out, usually by email from a senior banker to people he knows. The seller's problem here is a trade-off between price and secrecy. Breadth finds the highest price, and narrowness keeps the sale quiet, which matters when employees, customers and competitors would all behave differently if they knew. A middle-market business affordable to many buyers might be shown to two hundred parties, and a large or sensitive asset might be shown to eight.

A buyer who wants more signs a non-disclosure agreement, then receives the CIM. Meanwhile the bank builds a data room, a secure file system holding the prepared answers, with access staged. Early buyers see one layer and serious buyers see more. The sequencing is part of the design, and a buyer who has been through several of these reads the pacing as well as the files.

VTwo to four weeks

Management meetings

Buyers who are still interested meet the management team, and the bank rehearses the team first. A chief executive is usually excellent at running the company and has never had to describe it to a skeptical stranger holding a checkbook. The questions that land badly are predictable, so they are practiced. Management's credibility is part of what a buyer is pricing, which is why the meeting receives that preparation.

VIOne week, then a decision

First bids

Buyers submit an indication of interest, a number with almost no commitment that is not binding and will move. Its purpose is sorting. The number separates the buyers who want the business from those who were curious, and it gives the first reading of where the market is and not where the seller hoped it would be.

The bank then presents every bid to the owner with its own view of what the company is worth and which buyer it would prefer to deal with in two months. In Splunk's 2023 sale, Cisco's first indication was $130 a share in cash. The board's transaction committee judged it “not compelling” and told Cisco it did not reflect Splunk's standalone value (Splunk proxy statement).

VIIFour to six weeks

Final bids and negotiation

The field narrows, and the remaining buyers get deeper access and answers to specific questions. The senior banker's work here is hard to teach. He is on calls the seller is not on, telling a buyer what it would take to end the competition. A price above a certain figure might win exclusivity, meaning everyone else is sent home. He has to make each buyer believe it can win at a price good for the seller without pushing so hard that a buyer leaves and the process has one fewer bidder.

With one bidder the same work becomes a negotiation, and the Splunk record shows it in detail. Cisco offered $142 on 25 August 2023. Splunk countered at $168. Cisco moved to $146, Splunk to $160, and Cisco to $152 and then $156 on 30 August, when Splunk indicated it would accept $158. Cisco made a verbal offer of $157 on 31 August and proposed exclusivity. In six days the gap between the sides closed from $26 to $1.

Binding offers then arrive, in a letter of intent, and one is accepted.

VIIITwo to four months

Diligence and completion

The winning buyer now does something the seller rarely enjoys. Having agreed a price, it looks for reasons the price should be lower. That is not bad faith. It is what confirmatory diligence is for, and if the preparation in the second stage was done properly there is little left to find. Where something is found, it is negotiated into the purchase agreement, a long document that specifies what adjusts the price, how much cash stays in the business and what happens to the management team.

The agreement also allocates the risk that the deal will not close. Splunk's agreement provided for a $1 billion fee payable by Splunk to Cisco in specified circumstances and $1.478 billion payable by Cisco to Splunk in others. Signing came on 20 September 2023, followed by regulatory filings and, in Cisco's case, completion on 18 March 2024. Afterward the junior team documents the transaction, which takes longer than anyone expects, because the record becomes the credential the bank shows the next client.

When the sale is not an auction

The sequence above describes a competitive sale, and Splunk's was not one. Cisco first approached Splunk's chair in November and December 2021 and made an unsolicited indication of $165 to $175 a share on 17 December. Its indication rose to $193 in February 2022, Splunk's chair countered at $212, and Cisco withdrew the same day, citing concerns about Splunk's search for a chief executive. Cisco returned in April 2023, and after its $130 indication the board decided against running a market check because it judged that no other party was likely to be interested. On 1 September 2023 it decided again not to run one, so the proxy records no other buyer being contacted in 2023. The final price of $157 was below Cisco's own indication of $193 in February 2022.

The strongest objection to the argument of this page is that the standard sequence is a textbook and that real sales rarely follow it. I agree that this one did not, and I read the example as supporting the argument. The stages still describe the problems a seller has, and the board's decision to skip the competition was a judgment about one of them. A seller that skips the market check is accepting the risk that another buyer would have paid more, in exchange for secrecy and speed, and the shareholders who approved the deal were told so in the proxy.

A reader should therefore look for two things in any sale. One is the description of the process in the merger filing, called the Background of the Merger, which records who approached whom, how many buyers were contacted and whether a market check was run. The other is the price history in that section, because a final price that is lower than an earlier offer, as here, shows how much the conditions around the sale mattered. Failed or paused processes are common as well, and they appear in the record only when the company chooses or is required to disclose them.

Part Three. What this means if you are nineteen

The unit everyone measures is the wrong one

Everything above is about an institution. The people recruiting into this industry almost never are.

A student choosing between banks reads league tables, which rank firms by the value of the deals they advised on. The tables are real and they measure something. They also measure the wrong unit. A bank does not have relationships. The people inside it do, and Evercore says so to the SEC, listing the departure of senior professionals as a risk that could jeopardise its relationships with clients and result in the loss of client engagements and revenues. A firm that discloses its dependence on individuals in its own annual report is telling you where the asset sits.

Follow that through the stages above. The mandate in stage one comes from a managing director who has spent years in front of an owner with nothing to sell. The pitch is built by the team. The reason the pitch is being heard at all usually belongs to one person. If that person leaves, the pitches keep getting built and fewer of them get heard.

So the question a student is really asking, without knowing it, is not which bank. It is which five or six people.

Two analysts, same firm, different jobs

Two people can join the same firm in the same city in the same month and have unrecognisably different two years.

One lands in a group whose managing director is winning live sell-side mandates. That analyst spends the year inside the sequence above, turning a company’s records into something a stranger can read, sitting on the calls where bids come back, watching a board decide whether to run a market check. The other lands in a group whose coverage list is quiet and builds pitch books for meetings that do not convert. Same logo on the building, same training, same pay. Different job, and after two years a different set of things that person can credibly say they have done.

The industry does not hide this. It is invisible from outside because every source a student can reach is organised by institution. League tables are by institution. Rankings are by institution. Recruiting is by institution, and it has to be, since a firm hires for the whole firm and does not yet know which group will have the opening.

What to ask instead

Ask who the senior people in the group are and what they have closed in the past two years, by name. Ask how long they have been there. Ask who left recently and where they went.

These are polite questions and bankers usually answer them, because a good answer is a source of pride. A group that deflects has also answered.

The last thing worth knowing is that a league table is a lagging indicator of where people were. Bankers move, and their mandates follow on a delay of a year or two, which is roughly the length of an analyst programme.
