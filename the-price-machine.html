ThePrice Machine

$61BN FOR A BUSINESS THAT IS NOT
GOING TO GROW, WHICH IS THE POINT

Francis Ruan

Broadcom agreed to buy VMware in May 2022 for about $61 billion in cash and stock, into a market that was falling apart around it. Rates were climbing, the Nasdaq was in the process of losing a third of its value, and financing anything was becoming difficult. It closed in November 2023 after eighteen months of regulators.

What VMware does, briefly

VMware’s core product lets one physical server behave as many separate computers. That sounds like a technicality and it is the reason corporate computing has looked the way it has for twenty years.

It sits underneath an enormous amount of the software large companies run. Not visible, not exciting, and essentially impossible to remove without a project that would consume several years and somebody’s career.

The model, stated plainly

Broadcom is not a growth company and does not pretend to be. Its approach is consistent and it has worked for two decades.

Buy infrastructure software that large customers cannot realistically leave. Cut the research spending and the smaller product lines hard. Move customers onto larger, longer, more expensive contracts. Return the cash.

The industry is slightly squeamish about saying this out loud because it sounds like asset stripping. It is more precise than that. It is the observation that a product with genuine lock-in has been underpriced relative to what it is worth to the customer, and that nobody was going to fix that except an owner willing to be unpopular.

Here is what was in the horse

Everyone read the deal as an enormous bet made at a terrible moment. The thing in the belly was that the moment did not matter very much, because the return was not coming from growth or from the cycle. It was coming from raising the price of something customers had already decided they could not leave.

That is a return you can calculate before you sign. It is also why Broadcom could keep writing the cheque while the window shut on everybody else: it was not underwriting a forecast.

Within a year of closing, VMware customers were describing very large increases in what they paid. Some left. Most did the arithmetic on leaving and stayed, which was always the number that mattered.

My concern, and it is a real one

I want to be careful to separate what is observed from what I suspect.

Observed: the model is legal, repeatable and extremely profitable, and shareholders have done exceptionally well out of it.

My concern is narrower. A strategy that extracts from lock-in works until the memory of it changes behaviour, and enterprise buyers have very long memories. The cost of this does not appear in a quarterly number. It appears years later, in the deals a company is not invited to bid for.

Most acquirers buy a forecast. This one buys the distance between what a customer pays and what leaving would cost them.
