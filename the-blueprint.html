SOFTBANK PAYS $32BN FOR A COMPANY
THAT HAS NEVER MADE A CHIP

Francis Ruan

Masayoshi Son bought ARM Holdings in July 2016 for about $32 billion, a 43 percent premium, in cash, having apparently decided on it over a fortnight. The reaction was broadly that he had overpaid for a British company whose chips were in phones, at the exact moment phones stopped growing.

What ARM actually sells

ARM has never manufactured a chip and does not intend to. It designs the instruction set, the basic grammar a processor understands, and licenses it to everybody else. Apple, Qualcomm, Samsung and several hundred others take the design, build their own chip around it, and pay ARM a royalty on every single one.

The royalty is small. Often cents. The volume is roughly thirty billion chips a year, which is how cents become a business.

This is an unusual thing to own. ARM does not compete with its customers, which is why its customers were willing to standardise on it. It has no factories, so it has no capital cycle and no exposure to whether any particular phone sells. It sits underneath the industry and takes a toll.

Which is why the phone framing missed it

Everyone priced ARM as a mobile company because in 2016 almost all its royalties came from mobile. That was a description of the present read as a forecast.

What ARM actually had was a design that was radically more power efficient than the alternative, in a decade about to discover that power was the binding constraint on everything. A chip that uses less electricity is merely nice in a phone. In a building containing a hundred thousand of them, it is the entire economics.

Here is what was in the horse

Within a few years ARM designs were in the data centre, which had been Intel’s uncontested territory for twenty years. Amazon designed its own ARM-based server chips. Apple moved its laptops onto ARM and the comparison was not close. Nearly every AI accelerator built since has ARM somewhere in it.

SoftBank did not buy a mobile company. It bought the position underneath whatever came next, and it was able to buy that position cheaply precisely because everyone was busy pricing what had already happened.

ARM listed publicly again in 2023 at a valuation several times what SoftBank paid, with SoftBank keeping most of it. Whatever you think of the man, that one was read correctly.

What I take from it

The useful habit here is not about chips. It is that a business which charges a very small amount for something everybody has to use is almost always underpriced, because the market prices the current volume and not the position.

My read is that this is the single most repeated mistake in technology valuation, and it is repeated because the correct answer always looks expensive at the time.

Everybody valued what ARM was being paid for. One buyer valued where it was standing.
