Trojan Horses in Tech Banking

Two things in this industry look like ordinary business, and I think they are not. Both are being watched closely. Both are filed under the wrong heading, which is the most reliable way for something large to go unnoticed.

A note on what this is. Most of this site is reporting, with filings behind the numbers. This page is not. It is an argument built on two facts I believe are solid and a chain of reasoning on top of them that could break in several places. I have tried to say where.

The site is named after something that was brought inside because it looked like something else, and I use the phrase narrowly here. A Trojan horse in this sense has nothing to do with surprise. It is a development that gets correctly observed, correctly measured, and assigned to the wrong department, so that nobody responsible for the consequence is the one watching it.

IFiled as a credit story

The lender is changing, and the adviser follows

Start with something I did not make up. In 2002, four economists writing for the Federal Reserve Bank of New York found that acquirers tend to hire the banks that already lend to them, and tied that choice to wanting access to the money that pays for the deal. It is the most useful sentence I have read about how this industry actually works, and I wrote about it elsewhere on this site.

Now put it beside something everyone already knows. Over the past decade a great deal of corporate lending has moved out of banks and into funds. Apollo, Blackstone, Ares and others hold loans that would once have sat on a bank balance sheet. This gets reported constantly, and it gets reported as a credit story, about yield and about what happens in a downturn.

Here is the part I think sits in the wrong category.

If the advisory mandate follows the lending relationship, and the lending relationship is leaving the banks, then the mandate is leaving too, on a delay. The funds would not have to become better advisers for this to happen. They would only have to become the institution that already has the client’s number, for the reason that turned out to matter.

Several of them are building the rest of the capability. The large alternative managers have been adding capital markets teams for years. The usual description is that they are keeping fees they used to pay away, and that is true. I think it is the smaller half. The larger half is that every one of those teams is another reason for a chief executive to call a fund instead of a bank.

What would have to be true for me to be wrong. The relationship that produces a mandate might belong to a person rather than to an institution, in which case bankers simply move and carry the advantage with them, which is what already happens between banks. Or the funds might find that advising creates conflicts they do not want, since a firm that lends, owns and advises in one sector has a harder conversation than a bank does. I think the first objection is strong. I think the second is weaker than it sounds, because the industry has lived comfortably with the bank version of that conflict for decades.

IIFiled as a question about juniors

The apprenticeship is the product

The second one is harder to argue and I believe it is the larger of the two.

Every bank is automating the work juniors do. Document review, first-pass diligence, comparable company screens, formatting, the mechanical parts of building a model. This gets discussed as a question about junior employment, and the question people ask is how many analysts a bank will need.

I think that is the wrong question. The interesting one is about 2045.

A managing director’s value is judgment and relationships. Neither can be bought in, which is why the hires that move them are reported like transfers. Both are grown, and the growing medium is roughly fifteen years of doing the work badly, then adequately, then well. An analyst builds several hundred comparable company analyses and somewhere in there stops producing them and starts having a view about which comparison is dishonest. Nobody designed that as a training programme. It is a by-product of needing the work done.

Remove the work and the by-product goes with it.

Banks are not blind to this, and they do talk about training. But a training programme that exists on purpose is a cost, and a training programme that falls out of revenue-generating work is free. The free kind is the only kind that has ever produced a managing director. Replacing it means a bank spending real money every year for a benefit that arrives long after the people approving the budget have retired.

The measure I would watch, then, is training budget rather than headcount.

Where this could be wrong. Judgment might be more portable than I think, and senior people might keep arriving from somewhere else, the way they already arrive from competitors, although that only works while somewhere else is still producing them. Or the tools might compress the time it takes to develop judgment rather than removing the chance to develop it. That would be the good outcome and it is not far-fetched. I put real weight on it. I am describing a risk here, not a forecast.

What the two have in common

Both are measured accurately, by people whose job is a different question. Private credit is tracked by credit teams. Automation is tracked by operations and by recruiting. Neither sits with the people who think about where mandates will come from in a decade.

That is the whole mechanism, and it is not special to banking. An institution notices the thing. It files the thing correctly, for the purpose of whoever noticed it. Then the consequence lands somewhere nobody was assigned to look.

I would rather be wrong about both of these than right about either. The second one in particular describes a cost that falls on people who are currently in secondary school, and the decisions that determine it are being made now by people who will not be there to see it.

The Federal Reserve Bank of New York finding is from Allen, Jagtiani, Peristiani and Saunders, February 2002, and describes a historical sample. The growth of private credit and the build-out of capital markets teams at alternative managers are widely reported. Everything built on top of those facts is my own reasoning and should be read as argument rather than as reporting.
