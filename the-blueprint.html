SoftBank andArm

WHAT A COMPANY THAT MAKES NOTHING SELLS,
AND WHY IT IS DIFFICULT TO DISPLACE

Francis Ruan

SoftBank agreed to acquire Arm Holdings in July 2016 for about GBP 24.3 billion, roughly $32 billion, at 1,700 pence a share in cash. That was a premium of approximately 43 percent to the previous close. Arm has never manufactured a chip.

The business

Arm designs the instruction set a processor is built around, which is the basic vocabulary the chip understands, along with reference designs for the processor cores themselves. It licenses both to other companies.

A licensee such as Apple or Qualcomm pays an upfront fee for access to the design, then a royalty on each chip it ships. The royalty is typically a few cents. At the time of the acquisition the industry was shipping well over fifteen billion Arm-based chips a year.

Two things make that position durable. Software compiled for one instruction set does not run on another without being rebuilt and retested, so an industry that has standardised on a design carries a real cost to move away from it. And because Arm does not manufacture chips, it does not compete with the companies licensing from it, which mattered to licensees competing fiercely with each other.

The price, and what it required

A 43 percent premium paid in cash is a considerable commitment. At the time the large majority of Arm's royalty revenue came from mobile devices, and smartphone shipment growth had slowed to around three percent that year from roughly ten percent the year before.

The criticism at announcement was straightforward. SoftBank had paid a high price for a business whose main end market was maturing. On the information available, that was a reasonable thing to say.

What happened afterward

Arm designs were subsequently adopted well outside mobile devices. Amazon built server processors on them for its own cloud, and Nvidia used an Arm-based design for the processor paired with its accelerators. By 2026, industry figures put Arm-based machines at a substantial share of data centre server revenue, although at a much lower share of unit shipments. Those two measures differ enough that it is worth knowing which one you are being shown.

SoftBank listed Arm on Nasdaq again in September 2023 at a valuation considerably above what it had paid, retaining most of the company.

I would not read this as evidence that SoftBank anticipated demand for AI infrastructure. The public case made in 2016 concerned connected devices generally. What the purchase did require was a judgement that the licensing position was more durable than the end market it then served, and that judgement was correct.

A licensing business can be worth more than its current end market suggests, because the royalty follows the design wherever the design is adopted next.
