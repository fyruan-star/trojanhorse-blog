Trojan Horse of the Week31 October 2026

Oura Sleeps On Its IPO

Eight days on the road

Picture the morning of Tuesday 29 September 2026. For eight days a ring company that began in Finland and now lives in San Francisco had been walking investors through its Nasdaq debut. Fifty million shares at $40 to $44 each. Seventeen banks standing behind them. A ticker symbol, OURA, waiting at the exchange, and a pricing due within the week. Then, in place of a price, came a press release. The company would wait. I love this part of the story. Oura makes a ring whose whole job is to tell five million paying members how well they slept, and on the morning it counted, the company decided to sleep on it.

The demand had looked excellent. Eli Lilly had indicated an interest in up to $100 million of shares and funds affiliated with Dragoneer up to $300 million (the amended filing), non-binding but together about a fifth of the $2.1 billion on offer at the midpoint, and reports put the whole book at four to five times the shares available. The press release gave one reason, uncertainty in the IPO market. The offering documents show the rest of the room.

The gift

Every Trojan horse has the same parts. Something is welcomed by people who have good reason to like it, it looks like a gift, and something else rides inside. Here the horse was the offering itself. The people welcoming it were the buyers in the book and, in spirit, anyone who wears the ring. Suppose a woman in Austin, who exists only in this story, has worn one for two years. She takes it off to wash the dishes and puts it back on before she forgets. Every morning it grades her on an exam she does not remember sitting, and she has come to trust it the way people trust a friend who has seen them at their worst.

The outside of the horse was lovely. The S-1 shows paid members up from 2.9 million at the end of fiscal 2025 to 5.0 million on 30 June, revenue of $1.21 billion for the nine months, up 74%, and net income of $60.8 million against $1.6 million a year earlier. Historically more than 94% of ring activations become paid members, and about 85% of members are still paying after a year. A company people love, growing fast and making money, walks up to the gate of the public market and knocks, and of course the city comes out to look.

Whose money

Every dollar in an IPO has a return address, and the offering documents print them. An IPO can sell two kinds of shares. Primary shares are new, the company issues them, and the money goes to the company, which makes that part a fundraise. Secondary shares already exist and belong to founders, employees and early investors, and the money goes to them, which makes that part a cash-out. Picture a restaurant printing new seats to fund a second location, next to the original investors selling the seats they already hold.

Oura’s offering was 13.5 million primary shares and 36.5 million secondary ones (the amended filing), so 73% of it was existing holders selling. At the $42 midpoint that comes to roughly $567 million for the company and $1.53 billion for the sellers, per Fortune. IFR reported that three venture firms stood behind the 36.5 million, that Forerunner Ventures planned to sell all 28.7 million of its shares, which comes to about $1.2 billion at the midpoint, and that Lifeline Ventures planned to sell the 7 million it had left.

The company’s own $567 million had a return address too. Oura planned to use about $526.4 million of its estimated $532.6 million in net proceeds to pay tax withholding on employee stock. Many employees are paid in restricted stock units, a large batch vests when a company lists, and the employees owe tax on it. Suppose an engineer who joined early, invented for this story, holds stock that vests on the day of the listing. The tax falls due that same day, and the company uses cash from the new shares it sells to pay it on her behalf, which the filing calls the RSU net settlement. That leaves about $6 million for everything else, so on an offering of roughly $2.1 billion the money available to grow the business was about $6 million.

The accounts point the same way. The filing treats buying back preferred shares from holders for more than their carrying value as a deemed dividend, and it records $985.0 million of one in the nine months, which is why $60.8 million of profit appears as a $924.3 million loss attributable to common stockholders.

The reasons holders sell are ordinary and human. A venture fund has a fixed life and has to return money to the people who invested in it. An employee who has held stock for years owns most of her wealth in the shares of one company, and selling lets her turn one concentrated bet into something spread across many. IFR reported that Oura did not need to list now, since it is profitable, but that its shareholders were keen to cash out. Calling the offering a fundraise is like calling a retirement party a catering order.

The price tag

Beside the cash-out rode a price. At the top of the range Oura’s fully diluted valuation would have been $15.6 billion, against $11 billion in its last private round (IFR), and about eleven times the $1.43 billion of revenue for the twelve months to June (IPOScoop for the revenue), by my arithmetic. Software companies earn multiples like that because each extra customer costs almost nothing to serve. Oura’s revenue says something different. About 80% of it still comes from selling rings, down from 84% a year earlier, and gross margin was 55% in the nine months to June after 65% in fiscal 2024. The membership is the jewel of the filing, with revenue of $240.5 million, up 121%, at an 89% gross margin, and it is one fifth of the company.

Kat Liu of IPOX told Fortune the valuation can be justified if Oura is seen as an AI-enabled digital-health platform and looks hefty if it is seen as a ring maker, and she noted that sales still lean toward hardware. IFR reported that the banks bridged the gap with 2028 projections, a mid-4x multiple of revenue against about 5x for a comparison group that included Apple, Garmin, Netflix and Spotify, assuming 45% annual revenue growth through 2028 against 15% for the group. That resembles buying a house on the strength of the architect’s drawings of the extension, from people who are selling most of the house.

The ghosts at the gate

Wall Street has a long memory and a short list of hardware companies whose subscription stories ended badly. GoPro priced at $24 in 2014 and Peloton at $29 in 2019, and Fortune reported them down more than 94% and 82% from those prices. Gil Luria of D.A. Davidson told Fortune that investors have long memories, and Jay Ritter of the University of Florida pointed to single-product companies whose growth stalled after listing. People loved both products. The hardware economics showed once growth slowed.

The size of the market raises the same worry. The amended filing says Oura represented about 2% of annual global wearable shipments in the twelve months to June, citing IDC’s count of 212 million wearables. Luria noted that Apple would only have to decide to make a ring, and Apple sells none today. Oura has real answers, among them the 85% retention, a patent win that barred Ultrahuman and put RingConn on a license that includes royalties, and a lead in rings. A buyer weighing those against a hardware history can reasonably ask for a discount until the retention has been tested by time.

What the book really said

Four to five times covered sounds like proof of demand, and it proves less than it seems. An order book is a list of prices as much as a list of shares. Institutions often submit orders with limits, so a buyer can be large at $38, smaller at $42 and absent at $44. A restaurant can have a waiting list four times its seating and still seat nobody at $44 a plate.

Fortune relayed Bloomberg’s report that some prospective buyers stayed out over concern about the valuation, and IFR added that investors objected to a loose lock-up, which would have let early backers sell in waves soon after listing, with ten rolling releases from 35 days and a full release after 180 days. The buyers had read the return addresses too.

What Oura works out

The company has given one reason, the market, and Fortune’s experts doubted it, noting that the S&P 500 was up more than 13% for the year and within 1% of its record. Holtec Nuclear, Bamboo Insurance and Amaero had also pulled or shelved listings that month. The reading below is mine, built from the structure of the offering, and it needs only arithmetic.

Start with the cash. Almost all of the company’s own proceeds were spoken for by employee taxes, so a lower price would have cost the company almost nothing it planned to spend on growth. A lower price would have cost the sellers a great deal, because the discount comes out of what early holders receive for the same stock. The buyers wanted that discount and the sellers did not want to give it. The party in the room that loses little by waiting is the company, which is profitable and did not need the money to operate. Tom Hale said Oura had “the luxury of choosing our moment,” and the structure shows what that luxury is made of: a profitable company that needs none of the money, standing between sellers who want a price and buyers who want a discount. The sellers’ wish to diversify is real and belongs to them, and the company is the party that can afford to say not yet.

What to read first

Some outlets have called the move a withdrawal, and the company and most of the financial press that covered it call it a postponement. It has given no new date, and the filing remains on the SEC’s site.

For a founder, an IPO is a fundraise and a payday for early backers at the same moment, and how the two are split tells every buyer what to think. A company that does not need the money can wait for the price it wants, which is what Oura’s profit let it do. For an investor, three lines come before the pitch: how many shares are new, how many belong to insiders, and where the company’s own money goes. The next time Oura opens its gate, those three numbers are where to look first.
