Trojan Horse of the WeekWeek of 26 October 2026

Can P&G Keep Thorne’s Customers Loyal?

A loyalty that never signed

A company can be bought on a Tuesday, and its customers cannot. P&G agreed on 4 August 2026 to pay $3.8 billion for Thorne, about five and a half times the roughly $680 million L Catterton paid in 2023, for a company that Marketing Dive reports passed $500 million in annual revenue (the prices and dates are set out in the earlier piece on this deal). Buying a restaurant buys the lease, the ovens and the chairs. It does not buy the regulars, who remain free to inspect the chairs and leave.

That is the Trojan horse in this transaction. Loyalty is the reason the price is what it is, which makes it the part of the purchase most warmly welcomed, and also the part the buyer cannot take delivery of. It belongs to people who signed nothing, who were never consulted, and who can withdraw it without telling anyone.

The Michelin test

Suppose a friend of yours, known mainly for his Kraft macaroni and cheese, announces that he has bought a Michelin-starred restaurant. You will go, probably within the month, and before you sit down you will look at the menu for the blue box. This is no insult to him. He may be a fine cook with access to the best supply chain in the world, and making something that millions of people trust every day is a real form of excellence and not a lesser one. The inspection is what happens when a person holds an opinion about a place that was formed under different management.

P&G is in the friend’s position and Thorne’s customers are in yours. The owner’s competence is not in doubt. What customers want to see is that the thing they came for is still on the plate.

Who will decide

The people who decide this are not the people reading about it. Mergers and acquisitions are followed by bankers, investors and trade journalists, a crowd that fits in one conference hotel. A person who buys Thorne magnesium learns of the sale, if at all, from the bottle, so the information that reaches them is the price, the label, how the capsule agrees with them and whether the certification is still printed on the side.

Thorne’s own research, as Marketing Dive reports it, found consumers overwhelmed and confused by the market, and found that its messages about scientific rigor mattered less to them than the company had assumed. That suggests most buyers steer by a few visible cues, and a change nobody can see, a cheaper input for example, would pass most of them by. The customers placed to notice are the minority who can verify, meaning practitioners, athletes and heavy users. A brand with a practitioner heritage relies on them for a credibility that everyone else borrows, rather as a table borrows the one friend who can read the wine list. The sources do not give the split between the two groups, so this part is inference.

What people are saying

A Flow Space roundup of online reaction to the deal reports commenters split. Some hope it makes Thorne cheaper and easier to find, others fear reformulation and lower quality, and some point to P&G’s past recalls.

It is the worry recorded when P&G bought New Chapter in 2012. About a year afterward Nutritional Outlook quoted John Barrymore, who helped broker that sale, saying consumers worry that an acquirer might change product ingredients to make them cheaper. A worry about incentives is the hardest kind to answer, because nothing has happened for the owner to point to and the only rebuttal is time. No change has been announced here. The one statement from Thorne that I could verify is general. Its chief executive, Colin Watts, said the company wants to expand with P&G while keeping to its values and standards (NutraIngredients), and I found no specific commitment on formulation or certification.

What P&G has done before

New Chapter is the nearest thing to a controlled experiment. P&G bought it in 2012, when its sales were widely reported at about $100 million a year. After a year, Nutritional Outlook reported, P&G had added staff for quality control and innovation, the article noted no change in price, and the brand had not been pushed into other channels despite requests from mass-market retailers. The founder, Paul Schulick, said P&G was keeping its promise to leave the brand standalone.

Philosophers have an old puzzle about a ship whose planks are replaced one at a time until none of the originals remain, and whether it is still the same ship. Customers use a shorter test, which is whether anyone noticed. In 2018 Paul and Barbi Schulick left, citing “financial pressures to accelerate profits,” and Paul said that pressure could lead to products losing their distinctiveness (NutraIngredients). The report describes no specific change to formulation, quality, price or distribution, so as far as the record shows the planks stayed where they were. What changed was the shipwrights, who left six years in with profit pressure as their stated reason.

The other precedent critics cite is the December 2021 recall of aerosol dry shampoo and dry conditioner sprays sold under six names, among them Pantene, Aussie and Herbal Essences, after benzene was found in some of them. P&G recalled them as a precaution and stated that the benzene came from the propellant and not from an ingredient, that the levels detected were not expected to cause adverse health consequences, and that it had received no reports of adverse events (FDA notice). That is a different failure from a cost-driven reformulation. It is a supplied input failing at scale, and it bears on a brand whose reputation rests on testing.

Why it is close to even

The record supports both outcomes. In P&G’s favor, it left New Chapter standalone and added quality staff in the first year, and Marc Brush, a consultant and former editor of Nutrition Business Journal, says P&G has done a better job than most buyers of maintaining brand equity in supplement acquisitions. Against, Brush says that at this price Thorne has to keep growing quickly, that most marquee deals decline over time, and that the daily decisions which protect quality and trust are difficult to defend as years pass.

A price that requires fast growth tempts cost cutting and a faster pace of profit, which are the two things customers fear. It also makes loyalty the only asset worth guarding, since a premium wellness brand does not grow by losing the people who trust it. Both pressures sit on the same desk, and the record does not show which will win.

What would settle it

Nobody holds a press conference to announce that they have switched magnesium, so a loss of loyalty would arrive as a slow dip in repeat purchases that P&G sees first. Thorne stopped filing public reports when the 2023 tender closed, and after the sale it will sit inside P&G’s health care business, which P&G reports in aggregate. Outsiders would see it only through third-party sales data or the segment results.

Three things can be checked after the deal closes without access to internal figures. The first is whether Thorne keeps its NSF Certified for Sport certification, which Thorne says covers more than 20 of its products and not its whole range (Thorne). The second is whether prices or pack sizes change on the products existing customers buy most. The third is whether the practitioner relationships that gave the brand its credibility survive, since those customers are the ones able to tell the difference. Jones Day expects the deal to close in the fourth quarter of 2026 (Jones Day).

Prices and dates come from Thorne’s announcement and from the deal record published by P&G’s counsel. The account of consumer reaction rests on a published roundup that I could read only as a search summary, because the original is closed to automated readers, and on trade reporting of the reaction to the earlier New Chapter purchase. I could not read Reddit or other forums directly, so this is not a count of posts. The reasoning about who decides the outcome is mine.
