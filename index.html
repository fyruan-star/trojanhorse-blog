This journal argues that the facts which decide whether a technology deal works are rarely the ones its announcement leads with. Announcements describe growth and strategy. The outcomes in the deals examined here follow more closely from three other things: what it costs a customer to leave the product, whether the asset’s value depends on staying independent of its buyer, and who controls scarce inputs such as manufacturing, electricity and capital.

The name comes from the Greek story. The Trojans brought a wooden horse inside their walls because they believed it was an offering from the departing Greeks, and the soldiers hidden inside opened the gates at night. The analogy has fixed parts. Something is welcomed by someone who sees a benefit in it, and a risk enters with it that does its harm after admission. I use it only where a development fits those parts. The Trojan Horses page applies it to two cases in banking, in which banks welcome private credit partners and AI tools for real benefits that may weaken their access to clients and their ability to train advisers.

Ten Years tests the argument against technology transactions from 2016 to 2026. The Mandate and The Process explain how banks win and run those transactions. Each piece states its thesis first, names the view it disagrees with, and links its sources.
