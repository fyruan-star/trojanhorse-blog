Broadcom andVMware

A PURCHASE WHOSE RETURN DOES NOT DEPEND
ON THE ACQUIRED BUSINESS GROWING

Francis Ruan

Broadcom agreed to acquire VMware in May 2022 for approximately $61 billion in cash and stock plus assumed debt. It was announced as interest rates were rising sharply and completed in November 2023 after review in several jurisdictions.

What VMware sells

VMware's core product allows one physical server to run as several independent machines. That sounds like a technical detail and it is the basis on which a great deal of corporate computing has been organised for two decades.

It sits beneath the applications a company runs rather than beside them. Replacing it means moving every workload that depends on it, testing each one, and accepting the risk of the migration. Most organisations conclude the project would cost more than the licences.

The acquirer's method

Broadcom's approach is consistent and visible across CA Technologies, Symantec's enterprise business and now VMware. It acquires established infrastructure software, reduces research spending and discontinues smaller product lines, focuses commercial effort on the largest customers, and raises prices.

The significant feature is that the return does not require the acquired business to grow. It requires existing customers to stay, which the previous section explains. That is a calculation a buyer can make with more confidence than a growth forecast, and it is part of why Broadcom could proceed while financing conditions deteriorated for others.

After completion, customers reported substantial increases in what they pay and some moved to alternatives. Both outcomes were anticipated in the model. The question was always the ratio between them.

What I would hold open

That the approach is profitable is observable across twenty years of Broadcom's results, and I would not argue with it.

What is not yet settled is whether it carries a delayed cost. Enterprise buyers have long memories and considerable discretion about who they invite to bid next time. Such a cost, if it exists, would not appear in a quarterly figure. It would appear years later in processes the company is not part of, and by then it is difficult to attribute.

Where customers cannot leave cheaply, a buyer can underwrite price increases with more confidence than it can underwrite growth.
