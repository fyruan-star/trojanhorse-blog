Sell-side M&A

How a Company Gets Sold

A sale process is a sequence built to control what each buyer knows and when, and to make buyers compete. Each stage solves a specific problem for the seller, and a real sale shows which of those problems were present.

An owner could sell a business without help, and very few do. Selling requires knowing what the business is worth, who would want it and what separates it from the other companies a buyer is looking at that quarter. Those are full-time questions, and the owner already has a full-time job. So a bank is hired to run the process.

My argument is that the standard sequence below is best understood as a set of solutions. The bake-off solves the problem of choosing an adviser whose quality cannot be tested. Preparation and the documents solve the problem of making the company legible to strangers. Marketing, meetings and bids solve the problem of finding the highest price without losing control of the information. Diligence and completion solve the problem of keeping a price once it has been agreed. The sequence is the standard version, and it usually runs from three months to several years. Not every sale follows it, and the last section uses a sale that did not.

I Choosing the bank One to two weeks II Preparation Two to four months III The documents Runs alongside preparation IV Marketing Four to eight weeks V Management meetings Two to four weeks VI First bids One week, then a decision VII Final bids and negotiation Four to six weeks VIII Diligence and completion Two to four months

IOne to two weeks

Choosing the bank

Several banks are invited to present on why they should sell the company, an exercise the industry calls a bake-off. The problem it addresses is that the owner is paying for judgment whose quality cannot be checked in advance, so the pitch is a way to look for evidence of care and of understanding the business.

The outcome is often decided before the meeting. Senior bankers spend years in contact with owners who have nothing to sell, bringing them observations about competitors and about what similar businesses have fetched, so by the time a sale is real the owner often has a view already. The piece on how banks win the mandate gives the evidence for that.

That does not make the meeting ceremonial. A managing director is paid on the mandates brought in, so the pitch decides a large share of several people's income for the year, and the presentation is built by analysts and associates in a week or two. Small errors matter for a reason of inference and not of vanity. An owner reading a hypothetical deck with misaligned logos may conclude the firm would be equally careless with the sale. Banks lose these competitions, and two weeks of work ends with no fee at all.

IITwo to four months

Preparation

Once the bank has won, nothing is for sale yet. The problem now is that a buyer will not trust a profit figure the seller prepared itself. An independent accounting firm is therefore brought in to examine the company's earnings, in what is called a quality of earnings review, which checks that the profit is real, repeatable and not the result of one unusual year. It plays the role of a structural survey commissioned before listing a house, so that the buyer's own surveyor finds nothing the seller has not already seen.

While that runs, the bank assembles answers to the questions every buyer asks, which run to several hundred. They cover financial statements and projections, customer contracts, legal standing, litigation and how concentrated the customer base is. Some answers are so routine that their absence becomes the finding, because a buyer who asks for revenue by customer and does not receive it concludes either that the company does not know or that it would prefer not to say.

A hypothetical example shows the kind of work involved. A buyer wants to know customer concentration, repeat purchase rates and geographic spread. The company has never produced that analysis. What it can produce is a file of every transaction it has recorded, and someone spends two days turning it into three charts. The financial model is the visible deliverable, and much of the effort goes into converting the company's records into something a stranger can read.

IIIRuns alongside preparation

The documents

Three documents carry the sale, and they are written in a deliberate order of disclosure so that the seller reveals more only as a buyer shows more interest. The teaser is one or two pages and usually does not name the company. It describes the business well enough for a buyer to know whether it is interested, and not well enough to identify it. The confidential information memorandum, called the CIM, runs fifty pages or more and is the full argument: what the company does, why it wins, what the numbers have been doing and where growth is supposed to come from. The management presentation is the deck the chief executive uses to talk through the business in a meeting.

All three are marketing documents and also the company's formal argument about itself. Writing them well requires understanding both what the business does and what a particular kind of buyer is listening for.

IVFour to eight weeks

Marketing

The teaser goes out, usually by email from a senior banker to people he knows. The seller's problem here is a trade-off between price and secrecy. Breadth finds the highest price, and narrowness keeps the sale quiet, which matters when employees, customers and competitors would all behave differently if they knew. A middle-market business affordable to many buyers might be shown to two hundred parties, and a large or sensitive asset might be shown to eight.

A buyer who wants more signs a non-disclosure agreement, then receives the CIM. Meanwhile the bank builds a data room, a secure file system holding the prepared answers, with access staged. Early buyers see one layer and serious buyers see more. The sequencing is part of the design, and a buyer who has been through several of these reads the pacing as well as the files.

VTwo to four weeks

Management meetings

Buyers who are still interested meet the management team, and the bank rehearses the team first. A chief executive is usually excellent at running the company and has never had to describe it to a skeptical stranger holding a checkbook. The questions that land badly are predictable, so they are practiced. Management's credibility is part of what a buyer is pricing, which is why the meeting receives that preparation.

VIOne week, then a decision

First bids

Buyers submit an indication of interest, a number with almost no commitment that is not binding and will move. Its purpose is sorting. The number separates the buyers who want the business from those who were curious, and it gives the first reading of where the market is and not where the seller hoped it would be.

The bank then presents every bid to the owner with its own view of what the company is worth and which buyer it would prefer to deal with in two months. In Splunk's 2023 sale, Cisco's first indication was $130 a share in cash. The board's transaction committee judged it “not compelling” and told Cisco it did not reflect Splunk's standalone value (Splunk proxy statement).

VIIFour to six weeks

Final bids and negotiation

The field narrows, and the remaining buyers get deeper access and answers to specific questions. The senior banker's work here is hard to teach. He is on calls the seller is not on, telling a buyer what it would take to end the competition. A price above a certain figure might win exclusivity, meaning everyone else is sent home. He has to make each buyer believe it can win at a price good for the seller without pushing so hard that a buyer leaves and the process has one fewer bidder.

With one bidder the same work becomes a negotiation, and the Splunk record shows it in detail. Cisco offered $142 on 25 August 2023. Splunk countered at $168. Cisco moved to $146, Splunk to $160, and Cisco to $152 and then $156 on 30 August, when Splunk indicated it would accept $158. Cisco made a verbal offer of $157 on 31 August and proposed exclusivity. In six days the gap between the sides closed from $26 to $1.

Binding offers then arrive, in a letter of intent, and one is accepted.

VIIITwo to four months

Diligence and completion

The winning buyer now does something the seller rarely enjoys. Having agreed a price, it looks for reasons the price should be lower. That is not bad faith. It is what confirmatory diligence is for, and if the preparation in the second stage was done properly there is little left to find. Where something is found, it is negotiated into the purchase agreement, a long document that specifies what adjusts the price, how much cash stays in the business and what happens to the management team.

The agreement also allocates the risk that the deal will not close. Splunk's agreement provided for a $1 billion fee payable by Splunk to Cisco in specified circumstances and $1.478 billion payable by Cisco to Splunk in others. Signing came on 20 September 2023, followed by regulatory filings and, in Cisco's case, completion on 18 March 2024. Afterward the junior team documents the transaction, which takes longer than anyone expects, because the record becomes the credential the bank shows the next client.

When the sale is not an auction

The sequence above describes a competitive sale, and Splunk's was not one. Cisco first approached Splunk's chair in November and December 2021 and made an unsolicited indication of $165 to $175 a share on 17 December. Its indication rose to $193 in February 2022, Splunk's chair countered at $212, and Cisco withdrew the same day, citing concerns about Splunk's search for a chief executive. Cisco returned in April 2023, and after its $130 indication the board decided against running a market check because it judged that no other party was likely to be interested. On 1 September 2023 it decided again not to run one, so the proxy records no other buyer being contacted in 2023. The final price of $157 was below Cisco's own indication of $193 in February 2022.

The strongest objection to the argument of this page is that the standard sequence is a textbook and that real sales rarely follow it. I agree that this one did not, and I read the example as supporting the argument. The stages still describe the problems a seller has, and the board's decision to skip the competition was a judgment about one of them. A seller that skips the market check is accepting the risk that another buyer would have paid more, in exchange for secrecy and speed, and the shareholders who approved the deal were told so in the proxy.

A reader should therefore look for two things in any sale. One is the description of the process in the merger filing, called the Background of the Merger, which records who approached whom, how many buyers were contacted and whether a market check was run. The other is the price history in that section, because a final price that is lower than an earlier offer, as here, shows how much the conditions around the sale mattered. Failed or paused processes are common as well, and they appear in the record only when the company chooses or is required to disclose them.

Sources: the Background of the Merger section and the termination fee terms of Splunk's definitive proxy statement of October 2023, sec.gov. Stage durations are typical ranges and vary by transaction.
