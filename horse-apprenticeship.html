The second horse: automating the apprenticeship

Caterpillars Must Cocoon Before Becoming A Butterfly

AI could remove some of the work through which junior bankers develop the judgment those mandates require.

AI offers banks a different benefit. It can reduce the time required to retrieve information, summarize documents, and prepare initial analysis. Morgan Stanley introduced AskResearchGPT to help investment banking and other employees draw information from its research.

The optimistic argument is straightforward. Automate routine preparation, then give bankers more time for analysis, clients, and decisions.

Goldman Sachs’s George Lee described this possibility in a 2025 discussion about AI and employment. He suggested that “the quality of the apprenticeship rather than the quantity” could help firms “breed even better senior leaders.” In the same discussion, he acknowledged the challenge of developing future leaders if firms employ fewer juniors.

I think the optimistic outcome requires more organizational change than the productivity argument usually establishes. Completing a task faster creates available time. It does not determine how that time will be used or what an analyst will learn.

Banks themselves describe practical work as central to development. Goldman tells prospective interns: “Our interns don’t learn by watching, they learn by getting involved in real projects.”

Some of those projects contain tasks that AI can help perform. The difficulty is separating repetitive effort from useful practice.

An analyst preparing comparable company analysis for a software business must decide which companies belong in the group. Similar revenue growth can conceal differences in customer retention, profitability, or dependence on a few large contracts. An experienced banker’s corrections help the analyst understand which differences matter.

If AI prepares the analysis, the analyst may gain time to investigate those issues more deeply. But if the bank uses the savings to assign more projects to fewer people, the analyst may spend that time checking and forwarding outputs. The work gets completed while the opportunity to explain and defend the underlying decisions becomes smaller.

That creates a potential Trojan Horse inside the bank’s operating model. AI enters as a tool for improving productivity. If the bank responds by removing junior responsibilities without replacing their educational value, its adoption can weaken the process that produces experienced bankers.

The effect would take time to appear. Today’s senior bankers would continue advising clients. Current projects might finish faster. The consequences would become clearer as later cohorts reached positions requiring independent judgment.

The strongest objection is that AI could make learning substantially better. Juniors could test more assumptions, compare alternative approaches, and receive faster feedback. I think that is plausible. It supports a deliberate redesign of the apprenticeship.

A bank pursuing that outcome would require analysts to defend recommendations, explain changes to generated work, participate in client discussions, and review decisions with senior colleagues. Those activities need protected time and management attention. They cannot depend entirely on whatever time remains after the next assignment arrives.
