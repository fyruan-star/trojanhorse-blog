Third inLine

GOOGLE PAYS $32BN FOR SECURITY THAT
RUNS BEST ON ITS COMPETITORS

Francis Ruan

In July 2024 Wiz walked away from a $23 billion offer from Google, citing antitrust and investor concerns, with annual recurring revenue above $500 million and a plan to go public. Eight months later it sold to the same buyer, facing the same antitrust problem, for $32 billion. Thirty-nine percent more money.

What Wiz does, in plain terms

When a company moves into the cloud, it creates thousands of small settings. Who can reach this database. Which machines can talk to which. What is exposed to the open internet, usually by accident.

Wiz scans all of it and tells you which combinations are actually dangerous. Not a list of ten thousand warnings, which is what the previous generation of tools produced and which nobody reads. A short list of the paths an attacker could really walk.

The reason people liked it is unglamorous and decisive: it worked in an afternoon rather than a quarter.

Why Google wanted it so badly

Google Cloud has been a durable third behind Amazon and Microsoft for years, and third place in infrastructure is a specific kind of problem. You rarely win on price and you almost never win on features. You win when something forces a company to reopen a decision it made eight years ago.

In practice the thing that forces that reconsideration is security.

Here is what was in the horse

Wiz is deliberately multicloud, and Google committed publicly to keeping it working on Amazon, Microsoft and Oracle.

Read that as strategy rather than good manners. Google did not buy a feature for its own cloud. It bought the security layer sitting above everybody’s cloud, which means it now gets paid on the Amazon estate it was never going to win, and it holds the relationship with the security team, which is the team that gets consulted when workloads move.

It is the same structural position IBM bought with Red Hat seven years earlier, for within two billion dollars of the same money.

What the nine billion bought

The gap between the 2024 refusal and the 2025 agreement is the most instructive number here. Nothing about Wiz changed enough in eight months to justify $9 billion of additional value.

My read is that the increase was not a revaluation of the asset. It was the price of removing an alternative. Wiz had a real one, an IPO, and a business growing fast enough to make it credible, and a seller with somewhere else to go is the only seller who gets paid properly.

Whatever deal protections made an eighteen-month antitrust wait survivable for a company that had already said no once are in there too, and they are not public.

The premium was not paid for the company. It was paid for the fact that the company did not have to sell.
