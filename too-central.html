THE DEAL THAT DIED BECAUSE
ITS CUSTOMERS WERE THE BUYER’S RIVALS

Francis Ruan

NVIDIA agreed to buy ARM from SoftBank in September 2020 for about $40 billion in cash and stock. Seventeen months later it was abandoned, with NVIDIA forfeiting a $1.25 billion prepayment it had already handed over. It is the most instructive failure on this spine.

Why it made obvious sense

NVIDIA was becoming the most important chip company in the world. ARM owned the design language most chips are built from. Putting them together would have created something with a position in nearly every category of computing at once.

SoftBank wanted to sell, the price was large, and both boards agreed. On paper it is a clean transaction.

Why it was dead on arrival

ARM’s entire value rests on a single property: it is neutral. Its customers compete ferociously with each other and all of them are willing to build on ARM precisely because ARM does not build chips itself.

NVIDIA builds chips. It competes with substantially everybody on ARM’s customer list.

So the moment the deal was announced, every one of those customers had to consider the possibility that their foundation was now owned by a rival who could see their roadmaps and set their costs. Qualcomm objected publicly. Others did so privately and at length to regulators in the United States, the United Kingdom and the European Union.

Here is what was in the horse

The thing nobody in the announcement priced was that the asset could not survive being owned by that particular buyer. Not a regulatory risk in the ordinary sense, where you argue about market share. A structural one: the value of ARM was its independence, so a sale to any chipmaker destroyed some of what was being bought.

ARM listed separately in 2023 instead and has traded at a multiple of the $40 billion NVIDIA offered, which is the most expensive compliment ever paid to an antitrust lawyer.

SoftBank kept the $1.25 billion.

My read

There is a question worth carrying out of this and it almost never appears in a model, because it has no number attached.

Is any part of what you are buying made of the fact that you do not own it yet. Standards, platforms, benchmarks and marketplaces all have this property. They are valuable because they are trusted to be disinterested, and a disinterested asset held by an interested party is a smaller asset than it was the day before.

Some assets are worth less the moment you own them, and no spreadsheet has a row for that.
