Google andWiz

A seller with an alternative commanded a higher price

Francis Ruan

Wiz ended acquisition talks with Google in July 2024 at a reported $23 billion. Eight months later it agreed to sell to the same buyer for $32 billion, and the transaction completed on 11 March 2026 at a purchase price that Alphabet's filing puts near $29.5 billion. I think most of the difference is explained by Wiz having shown that it could say no, and that a seller with an alternative negotiates from a different position than one without.

What Wiz sells

A company that runs infrastructure in a cloud provider creates thousands of configuration settings, such as which systems can reach which databases, what is open to the internet and which credentials carry which permissions. Wiz examines all of it and reports which combinations create a usable path for an attacker. Earlier tools in the category produced long lists of individual warnings that teams stopped reading. Wiz produced short lists and could be connected and returning results within hours, which is most of why it grew quickly. Its reported annual recurring revenue was above $500 million in mid-2024, against $350 million a year earlier, figures that come from trade reporting and not from filings.

The refusal

In July 2024 Wiz's chief executive, Assaf Rappaport, wrote to employees that “saying no to such humbling offers is tough” and that “while we are flattered by offers we have received, we have chosen to continue on our path to building Wiz.” Reporting on the memo said it set an initial public offering and $1 billion in annual recurring revenue as the goals (Globes).

A refusal of that kind has two effects. It tells the buyer that the seller does not need the sale, and it gives the seller a visible alternative, a public listing, to which the buyer's price will be compared. The buyer's next offer has to beat a listing and not merely match its previous number.

Why the increase is not only growth

The offer rose by about 39 percent in eight months. Wiz kept growing over that period, and growth explains part of the increase. I infer that it does not explain all of it, because a refusal followed by a higher offer is the pattern a change in bargaining position produces. Terms covering the regulatory wait may also account for some of the gap. Those terms are not public, so the inference rests on the timing and on the seller's own statement and not on a disclosed breakdown of the price.

Google's reasons for paying are easier to state. Google Cloud has been third behind Amazon and Microsoft for several years. A provider in that position rarely wins on price and seldom on features alone, because what holds a customer in place is the cost of moving. Wiz works across Amazon, Microsoft and Oracle as well as Google, and Google committed publicly to keeping it that way. Software used regardless of which cloud a customer chose produces revenue from estates Google has not won.

The final figure

The $32 billion reported at announcement and the approximately $29.5 billion in Alphabet's quarterly filing for the period ended 31 March 2026 differ for ordinary reasons, including adjustments and the treatment of equity awards. The first figure was reported widely and the second much less so, so a reader comparing deals should check which of the two a number describes.

The strongest objection

The objection is that the increase reflects Google's own priorities and not Wiz's leverage. Google wanted a strong security product in a market where it trails, and a buyer in that position pays more for a strategic fit regardless of what the seller says.

The evidence that would separate the readings is whether other buyers were involved or were reported to be, and whether Wiz's price would have held without the refusal. The record I found does not answer either, and the claim here is limited to the timing and to Wiz's own statement.

What a reader should take from it

When a seller turns down an offer and later accepts a higher one, check what alternative it created in between. A listing, a competing bidder or a financing source all change the price, and they show up in the result before they show up in the company's performance.

  3. Rappaport's memo to employees, July 2024, as reported by Globes. The $23bn figure and the ARR figures are from trade reporting and not from filings.
