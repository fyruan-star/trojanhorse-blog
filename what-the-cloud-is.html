WhatThe Cloud Is

Renting computing created lock-in, and lock-in is the asset

Francis Ruan

The cloud is computing that a company rents by the hour from a provider that owns the machines. I think the most valuable result of that arrangement for the providers is lock-in, the cost a customer would face to leave, and that it explains why buyers pay large premiums for software that works across all of the providers. IBM's purchase of Red Hat and Google's purchase of Wiz are two examples.

What changed in 2006

Before the cloud, a company that needed computing bought computers, housed them, employed people to maintain them and paid up front for capacity it would mostly not use. Amazon, which had built a large amount of capacity for its retail business, began renting storage in March 2006 and computing capacity a few months later (AWS). Microsoft and Google followed.

In accounting terms a large one-off purchase became a monthly expense. That changed who could start a software company. Two founders could rent the same capacity as a bank and pay only for what they used, and a great many software companies founded afterward exist in a form that would not have been affordable before.

Why customers stay

Amazon arrived first and remains the largest. Microsoft came second and already sold software to most large organizations. Google has been third for most of the period the timeline covers. Once a company's systems and data are established with one provider, moving them is a project and not a decision. It takes a long time, costs a great deal and visibly improves little when it is finished.

The term for this is lock-in. A hypothetical customer with five hundred applications built for one provider's services would have to rewrite or retest each one to move. The provider's most valuable asset is the size of that bill.

Multicloud and the neutral supplier

Larger customers recognized their position and began keeping systems with more than one provider deliberately, so that no single supplier could raise prices without a credible response. The practice is called multicloud. It creates a position worth having for a software supplier whose product works across all three providers, because that supplier earns revenue regardless of which one a customer chooses.

IBM paid about $34 billion for a company in that position in 2018, and Google agreed to pay about $32 billion for another in 2025. Both purchases are about owning a neutral layer, and both carry the same risk, which is that the buyer is also a cloud provider and may weaken the neutrality it paid for.

The qualification

Lock-in is eroding at the edges. Google announced in January 2024 that it would waive data transfer fees for customers leaving its cloud, and Amazon and Microsoft followed within about two months. Reporting links the moves to new European Union rules, and the waivers carry conditions, so the change is partial (The Register, Light Reading).

What a reader should take from it

When a software company is sold, check whether its product works across the providers and whether the buyer owns one. If both are true, the price depends on the buyer preserving neutrality, and the useful evidence is how the product's support on competing clouds changes over the next several years.
