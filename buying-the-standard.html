Buying theStandard

FORTY PEOPLE IS THE WRONG
NUMBER TO BE STARING AT

Francis Ruan

Every write up of this deal reached for the same joke. Two billion dollars, roughly forty people, do the division, marvel at the price per head. I did the division too. Then I sat with it and decided the headcount is the least informative fact in the entire transaction, and that the people repeating it, myself included for about an hour, were solving the wrong problem.

What the acquihire framing misses

Tabular was founded by the engineers who created Apache Iceberg while they were at Netflix. Iceberg is an open table format, the unglamorous plumbing that decides how enormous piles of data get organised so different tools can read them. Call it a talent deal and you have described the receipt rather than the purchase.

Here is the fact that reframed it for me. Databricks maintains Delta Lake, which was Iceberg's principal rival. So Databricks did not buy a capability it lacked, and it did not buy forty engineers it could have hired one at a time for a great deal less. It bought the other side of a format war, along with the specific people whose names are the reason anyone trusts that format.

Why I think this is cheap, which is not the consensus

Two billion sounds absurd right up until you price the alternative. If the industry standardises on Iceberg and Databricks is outside it, then every enterprise data decision for the next decade begins with a format Databricks does not influence. That is not a competitive disadvantage, it is a gravity problem, and you do not fix gravity with a better product.

My read is that this was never about revenue or engineers. It was about buying the right to stop competing against your own standard, and about being able to say that both formats now sit under one roof. Standards are not won by being better. They are won by becoming the default, and defaults have a price. I think this one was under it.

The number nobody wants to look at

Databricks has confirmed publicly only that consideration exceeded one billion dollars. The two billion figure everyone quotes, mine included, is reporting.

I find that gap interesting rather than sinister. A company that was delighted with a price tends to say the price. A company that would rather you discussed the strategy gives you a floor and lets the number find its own level. That is not evidence of anything. It is just a thing I notice, and I notice it often enough that it has become a tell.

Where I would push back on myself

Iceberg is open, and the entire reason anyone trusts an open format is that no single commercial party steers it. The largest commercial party in the category now employs its creators.

I have seen no evidence of anything going wrong and I do not think capture was the intent. But an open standard maintained by its biggest beneficiary is a structure that works exactly as long as everyone keeps behaving well, and I am not willing to call that a permanent condition.

My call

Cheap if it ends the format war, expensive if the community reads it as a capture and routes around it. The checkable version of that question is whether Iceberg's contributor base stays genuinely broad over the next few years or quietly narrows to one company, and that is a thing you can go and look at rather than argue about.

They did not buy forty people. They bought the right to stop competing with their own standard.
