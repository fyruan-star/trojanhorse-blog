WhatA Bank Does

Advice and money are two businesses with different incentives

Francis Ruan

An investment bank does two things that are often described as one. It advises on transactions, and it arranges money. The incentives differ between them, and I think a reader who keeps the two apart can read an announcement's list of advisers as evidence about how a deal was framed and financed.

Advice means running a process

When a company is sold, somebody has to run the sale. That means deciding which buyers to approach and in what order, preparing the information they receive, setting a timetable and building the analysis that supports a view on what the business is worth. There is also a room of people to manage, each wanting a somewhat different outcome.

This is the sell-side. The adviser is paid a percentage on completion and very little if the sale does not happen, which is relevant when reading any adviser's view on whether a sale should proceed. The buy-side is the other position. The adviser represents the acquirer, on what to offer, how to structure it and how to avoid paying more than necessary in a process designed to encourage exactly that.

Arranging money

A company that needs capital can sell shares, which means giving up a portion of its ownership permanently, or borrow, which means committing to repay. Banks arrange both, and the choice between them says something about how a company views its own prospects.

Equity is ownership, and an initial public offering is the first sale of it to the public. Debt is a loan, repaid before shareholders receive anything. A convertible note is a loan the holder may exchange for shares on agreed terms, which lets the borrower pay a lower interest rate in return for possible dilution. A term loan B is a large floating-rate loan sold to investment funds and not held by banks, and the B describes where it ranks if the borrower gets into trouble.

Coverage

Banks organize themselves by industry. A coverage group is the team responsible for the relationship with companies in one industry. A technology coverage team follows the companies, reads the filings and is in contact well before there is a transaction to discuss. A company deciding whether to sell does not usually run a search for an adviser, and the relationships that determine who receives the call are built over years. This is why senior moves between banks are followed closely and why the value of such a hire is usually described in terms of the relationships it brings.

What the fee is for

The fee is a percentage, and the percentage falls as transactions get larger. On a very large deal it may be a fraction of one percent, which still amounts to tens of millions of dollars. Whether that is proportionate is negotiated on every transaction. What can be said is that errors are expensive in ways that are hard to see from outside. A company sold at the wrong time, to the wrong buyer, or on terms that fail in a regulatory review costs its owners far more than any fee.

What a reader should take from it

The work comes down to two questions: what the business is worth to a particular buyer, and where the money will come from. When reading an announcement, look at which banks advised each side and which arranged the financing, and ask whether the same institution did both. The separate piece on how banks win the mandate takes that question up.
