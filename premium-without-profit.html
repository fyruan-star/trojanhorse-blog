Premium withoutProfit

A HUNDRED AND SEVENTY PERCENT
FOR A COMPANY LOSING MONEY

Francis Ruan

I collect deals where the price refuses to make sense on the obvious metric, and this is one of my favourites. Mars offered five hundred and thirty four million pounds for Hotel Chocolat in November 2023, three hundred and seventy five pence a share in cash, which was a premium of roughly a hundred and seventy percent to the previous close. In the financial year to July 2023 Hotel Chocolat had gone from a pre tax profit of twenty one point seven million pounds to a loss of eight hundred thousand.

Start with what you cannot do here

You cannot value this on earnings, because at the moment of the offer there effectively were not any. Whatever Mars was paying for, it was not a multiple of profit, and that alone makes the deal more interesting than most of the list.

So the question becomes what does exist. Hotel Chocolat was founded in 2004, sells through its own stores and its own website, and owns a hundred and forty acre cocoa estate and the Rabot Hotel in Saint Lucia. That is a company that controls its product from the tree to the till.

My explanation

My read is that Mars bought a structure it could not build. Mars is extraordinary at manufacturing and distribution, which means it is extraordinary at selling chocolate through other people's shelves. What it does not have is a premium brand with its own estate, its own retail, and a direct relationship with the person eating the thing.

Vertical integration is slow and expensive to assemble and nearly impossible to acquire in pieces. When you decide you want it, you have to buy somebody who already did the twenty years of work. That is what a hundred and seventy percent premium is actually pricing, and once I framed it that way the number stopped looking irrational to me.

What makes me hesitate

The thing that makes Hotel Chocolat worth a premium is that it is small enough to control end to end. Every step Mars takes to scale it, more stores, more countries, more volume through the estate, is a step toward the model that made the premium worth paying evaporating.

My concern is not that Mars will damage the brand deliberately. It is that the instinct which makes Mars excellent, push volume through distribution, is precisely the instinct that dissolves a vertically integrated premium business. I have no evidence of that happening. It is simply the tension I would be watching.

My call

Defensible, and more interesting than the premium makes it look. The number I would track is not revenue growth, it is the share of sales still going through Hotel Chocolat's own channels. If that falls while total sales rise, Mars bought a brand and let go of the reason it was worth buying.

You cannot pay a multiple of earnings for a company that has stopped earning, so the premium is telling you what else was in the box.

Sources

  1. Mars offer announced 16 November 2023 at 375p per share; transaction completed 25 January 2024.
  2. Hotel Chocolat financial year to July 2023, pre tax result moving from a 21.7m profit to a 0.8m loss, per contemporaneous coverage of the company's results.
