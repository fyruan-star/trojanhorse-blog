Google andWiz

WHAT A SELLER WITH AN ALTERNATIVE IS WORTH,
AND WHAT THE FINAL FIGURE TURNED OUT TO BE

Francis Ruan

Wiz ended acquisition discussions with Google in July 2024 at a reported $23 billion, citing antitrust and investor concerns and stating an intention to go public. Eight months later it agreed to sell to the same buyer for $32 billion. The transaction completed on 11 March 2026, and Alphabet's quarterly filing puts the purchase price at approximately $29.5 billion after adjustments.

The product

When a company runs infrastructure in a cloud provider, it creates thousands of configuration settings. Which systems can reach which databases, what is accessible from the open internet, which credentials carry which permissions.

Wiz examines all of it and reports which combinations create a usable path for an attacker. Earlier tools in the category produced very long lists of individual warnings, which teams largely stopped reading. Wiz produced shorter lists, and could be connected and returning results within hours rather than over a deployment project. That is most of the reason it grew quickly.

Why Google was willing to pay

Google Cloud has been third behind Amazon and Microsoft for a number of years. A provider in that position rarely wins on price and seldom on features alone, because what holds a customer in place is the cost of moving rather than satisfaction.

Wiz works across Amazon, Microsoft and Oracle as well as Google, and Google committed publicly to keeping it that way. Software used regardless of which provider a customer chose produces revenue from estates Google has not won, and puts it in contact with the security teams consulted when a company considers moving workloads.

What the increase actually bought

The gap between $23 billion refused and $32 billion agreed is the most instructive figure here. Wiz's reported annual recurring revenue was above $500 million in mid-2024, against $350 million a year earlier, and it kept growing. That growth does not on its own account for the difference.

What Wiz had was a credible alternative. A company able to list publicly does not have to accept an offer, and a seller with somewhere else to go negotiates from a materially different position than one without. Deal terms addressing the regulatory wait are likely to form part of the explanation as well, and those are not public.

A note on the final figure

The $32 billion reported at announcement and the approximately $29.5 billion recorded on completion differ for ordinary reasons, including adjustments and the treatment of equity awards. The first figure was reported widely and the second much less so. It is a useful reminder to check whether a number describes an announcement or a conclusion.

A seller able to walk away is in a different negotiation from one that cannot, and the difference is visible in the price.
