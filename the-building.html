InsideA Data Centre

A property with an electricity contract, measured in megawatts

Francis Ruan

A data center is a building full of computers, and describing it that way understates how unusual the business has become. The scarce input is not the computers. It is the power and the land to put them on, so I think the operators that secured power early hold an advantage that is difficult to copy.

What is in the building

Rows of metal racks hold servers, and each server holds processors. Above them runs electrical distribution, and alongside them runs cooling, because nearly every watt delivered comes out as heat and processors fail when they get too hot. Most of the engineering difficulty is in moving electricity in and heat out, which is why the people building these facilities increasingly come from industrial construction and not from software.

Why these are measured in megawatts

Data centers are not usually described in square feet or in numbers of servers. They are described in megawatts. A megawatt is roughly the draw of eight hundred homes, and a large facility built for AI training may want several hundred of them. That is comparable to the demand of a small town, concentrated in one place and running close to full load through the night.

Water is a consideration as well, because a good deal of cooling works by evaporation. That has made siting a local political matter in regions where water is limited.

Why spending moved this way

The sequence is straightforward. A model needs many processors, many processors need a building, and the building needs power. Power requires a grid connection, and utilities grant connections on timescales measured in years. The constraint moved step by step toward the physical end of that chain and spending followed it. A growing share of capital that would once have gone to software development went to construction, electrical equipment and cooling.

Who benefits, and the qualification

An operator with a grid connection already in place can add computing faster than one that has to wait for a utility, and the position can be worth more than the buildings. A hypothetical buyer comparing two identical sites would pay more for the one with the connection already granted.

The qualification is that the advantage lasts only while connections are scarce. If utilities, regulators and equipment makers expand capacity faster than demand grows, the position loses value. The evidence to watch is the typical wait for a connection in the largest data center markets.

What a reader should take from it

Value a data center as a property with an electricity contract and not as a technology asset. The questions that matter are how many megawatts are contracted, on what terms and by when, and these are more informative than the number of servers.
