WhatTraining Requires

A software advance became a question about electricity

Francis Ruan

The part of the AI boom that mattered commercially to the companies in Ten Years was less the software than the computing it required. Training a large model is a physical activity that uses thousands of processors, buildings and large amounts of electricity, and the scarcest input moved step by step from processors to buildings to power. That sequence explains why storage, networking and construction attracted capital.

Training and inference

Training is building the model. A system is shown a very large quantity of text or images and adjusts billions of internal values until it predicts well. This runs on thousands of processors for weeks or months and is expensive. Inference is using the result. A question goes in and an answer comes out. Each instance is cheap and the total is not, because there are a great many questions.

Training resembles a construction project, and inference resembles a utility bill. Most of the spending described in the timeline between 2023 and 2025 is the first kind.

Why thousands of chips and not one fast one

A conventional processor performs operations one after another very quickly. A graphics processor performs thousands of simple operations at the same time. Nvidia began as a maker of graphics chips for games, where the color of millions of pixels has to be worked out at once and the order does not matter. Training a model has a similar shape, which is how a company whose products were first sold for video games came to supply much of the hardware used for AI.

One processor is not enough. A large training run uses thousands working together, so the connections between them determine how much of that hardware is in use at any moment. That is the reasoning behind Nvidia's purchase of Mellanox, which sold those connections, and behind the storage company DDN's valuation, since data has to reach the processors fast enough to keep them working.

Where the constraint ends up

Thousands of processors in one building draw a great deal of electricity and turn nearly all of it into heat, so the building needs power delivered and heat removed. Both are problems of civil and electrical engineering and not of software. The International Energy Agency projects global data center electricity use to reach about 945 terawatt-hours by 2030, roughly the current consumption of Japan, and expects data centers to account for nearly half of US electricity demand growth through 2030 (IEA, Energy and AI).

By 2026 the constraint most often described by operators was electrical capacity and the time needed to obtain a grid connection. In several markets hardware can be bought considerably faster than power can be connected.

The qualification

Projections of this kind depend on assumptions about how efficient models and chips become. If training needs substantially less computing for the same result, the constraint would ease and some of the spending in the timeline would look excessive in hindsight. Published IEA scenarios vary widely after 2030 for that reason.

What a reader should take from it

When a company on this subject announces a purchase or an investment, find which part of the chain it addresses, processors, connections, storage, buildings or power, and whether that part is the current constraint. The evidence that would change the picture is a sustained fall in the computing needed per model, which would weaken the case for paying a premium for any part of the chain.

  • InferenceUsing the model. Cheap individually, expensive in aggregate.
